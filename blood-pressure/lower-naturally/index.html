How to Lower Blood Pressure Naturally

Last updated: July 2026

Lifestyle changes are the first-line treatment for elevated blood pressure — and they work. Individually, each change below typically lowers systolic pressure by 3–11 mmHg; combined, they can rival a first medication. Here are the ten with the strongest evidence, with realistic expectations for each.

One important note before you start: if you're already on blood pressure medication, these changes complement it — never stop or reduce a prescription without your doctor.

1–3: Move more, weigh less, sit less

1. Get regular aerobic exercise

Around 150 minutes a week of moderate activity — brisk walking, cycling, swimming — typically lowers systolic pressure by 5–8 mmHg in people with hypertension. Consistency beats intensity: five 30-minute walks do more than one heroic workout. Strength training two days a week adds a further benefit.

2. Lose excess weight

Blood pressure usually falls by roughly 1 mmHg for every kilogram (about 2.2 lbs) lost. Waist size matters too: carrying weight around the middle is more strongly linked to hypertension.

3. Break up sitting time

Long unbroken sitting is associated with higher blood pressure even in people who exercise. Standing or walking for a few minutes each hour helps.

4–6: Fix the plate

4. Eat the DASH way

The DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) — vegetables, fruits, whole grains, low-fat dairy, legumes, nuts, less red meat and sugar — lowers systolic pressure by up to 11 mmHg in people with hypertension. It's the single most effective dietary change on this list. See our companion guide to foods that lower blood pressure.

5. Cut sodium

Most people eat far more salt than the recommended limit of 2,300 mg of sodium a day (about one teaspoon of salt); an ideal target for most adults with high blood pressure is closer to 1,500 mg. Cutting back typically lowers systolic pressure by 5–6 mmHg. Around 70% of dietary sodium comes from processed and restaurant food, not the salt shaker — reading labels beats skipping seasoning.

6. Add potassium

Potassium blunts sodium's effect. Bananas, potatoes, beans, spinach, and yogurt are rich sources. Aim for potassium from food rather than supplements — and check with a doctor first if you have kidney disease.

7–8: Limit what works against you

7. Drink less alcohol

Keeping alcohol within moderate limits — at most one drink a day for women, two for men — lowers systolic pressure by about 4 mmHg in people who drink more than that. Heavier drinking raises blood pressure reliably.

8. Quit smoking

Each cigarette raises blood pressure for many minutes afterward, and smoking accelerates the artery stiffening that drives long-term hypertension. Quitting has the biggest overall cardiovascular payoff of anything on this page, even if its direct effect on resting blood pressure is modest.

9–10: Sleep and stress

9. Sleep 7–9 hours

Short sleep and poor-quality sleep are linked to higher blood pressure, and untreated sleep apnea is one of the most common causes of resistant hypertension. If you snore heavily or wake unrefreshed, mention it to your doctor.

10. Manage stress deliberately

Chronic stress keeps blood pressure elevated through repeated surges and through its knock-on effects — poor sleep, alcohol, comfort eating. Slow-breathing exercises, meditation, and regular downtime each show measurable, if modest, reductions. Pick one you'll actually repeat.

Track the change — it's half the treatment

Lifestyle changes take 2–8 weeks to show up in your readings, and the effect is easy to miss in day-to-day noise. Measuring at home a few times a week, with proper technique, does two things: it shows you (and your doctor) whether the changes are working, and it keeps you motivated — a trend line drifting downward is powerful feedback.

Frequently asked questions

How quickly can I lower my blood pressure?

Some changes act within days (cutting sodium, stopping heavy drinking), but most take 2–8 weeks of consistency to show clearly in your average readings. Track weekly averages rather than judging by single measurements.

Can I lower blood pressure enough to stop medication?

Sometimes — people with mild hypertension who lose weight, change diet, and exercise do occasionally reduce or stop medication. But that decision is always your doctor's call, based on your monitored readings. Never stop on your own; rebound hypertension can be dangerous.

Does drinking water lower blood pressure?

Staying hydrated supports healthy blood pressure, and dehydration can cause swings in either direction, but drinking extra water is not a treatment for hypertension. The changes with real evidence are diet, sodium, exercise, weight, alcohol, sleep, and stress.

What lowers blood pressure the most?

Of single changes, the DASH diet (up to about 11 mmHg systolic) and regular aerobic exercise (5–8 mmHg) have the largest effects. Combining several changes is what produces medication-sized results.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Never start, stop, or change blood pressure medication without your doctor.
