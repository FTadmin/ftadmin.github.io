15 Foods That Help Lower Blood Pressure

Last updated: July 2026

No single food is a treatment for high blood pressure — but dietary pattern is one of the strongest levers you have, worth up to 11 mmHg of systolic pressure. The foods below earn their place through three mechanisms: potassium (which offsets sodium), dietary nitrates (which relax blood vessels), and minerals and compounds that support flexible arteries.

Think of this list as a shopping guide for the DASH diet, the eating pattern with the best blood-pressure evidence of all.

The 15 foods

  1. Leafy greens — spinach, kale, chard, arugula. Rich in potassium and nitrates; the backbone of nearly every blood-pressure-friendly plate.
  2. Beets and beet juice — the best-studied nitrate source; small trials show measurable systolic drops within hours of a glass of beet juice.
  3. Berries — blueberries and strawberries carry anthocyanins linked to lower blood pressure in long-term studies.
  4. Bananas — the famous potassium source (~420 mg each) and the easiest swap for a salty snack.
  5. Oats — beta-glucan fiber is associated with modest reductions in both numbers; a low-sodium breakfast win.
  6. Fatty fish — salmon, mackerel, sardines. Omega-3s at 2–3 g/day show real reductions; two servings a week is the practical target.
  7. Unsalted nuts and seeds — almonds, walnuts, pistachios; flax, chia, and pumpkin seeds bring magnesium and arginine.
  8. Beans and lentils — potassium, magnesium, and fiber in one package; swapping them for some red meat mirrors the DASH pattern.
  9. Plain yogurt and low-fat dairy — calcium and potassium; regular yogurt eaters show lower hypertension risk in cohort studies.
  10. Garlic — allicin modestly lowers blood pressure in trials, mostly at aged-garlic-extract doses; as a salt replacement it wins twice.
  11. Dark chocolate — cocoa flavanols produce small but consistent reductions; a square or two of 70%+, not a bar.
  12. Olive oil — polyphenol-rich extra virgin olive oil is a pillar of both DASH-style and Mediterranean eating.
  13. Pomegranate — juice trials show promising systolic reductions, though watch the sugar if drinking rather than eating it.
  14. Potatoes (plain) — one of the richest potassium sources per serving; baked or boiled, not fried or heavily salted.
  15. Herbs and spices instead of salt — basil, oregano, cumin, lemon, chili: the swap that makes every other item on this list sustainable.

The pattern that ties it together: DASH

The DASH diet is less a menu than a set of daily proportions: 4–5 servings of vegetables, 4–5 of fruit, whole grains, 2–3 low-fat dairy servings, nuts and legumes several times a week, limited red meat, sweets, and sugary drinks. In trials it lowered systolic pressure by 8–11 mmHg in people with hypertension — more when combined with sodium reduction.

You don't have to adopt it overnight. Adding one vegetable serving and swapping one processed snack for fruit or nuts, then iterating, is how it sticks. For the other levers — exercise, alcohol, sleep, stress — see how to lower blood pressure naturally.

What to limit

  • Sodium — the big one. Aim under 2,300 mg/day (about a teaspoon of salt); closer to 1,500 mg is better for most people with high blood pressure. Roughly 70% hides in processed foods: bread, cured meats, cheese, sauces, canned soups, takeaway.
  • Alcohol — more than moderate drinking raises pressure reliably.
  • Sugary drinks and heavily processed food — linked to higher blood pressure independent of weight.
  • Licorice — real licorice (glycyrrhizin) can raise blood pressure substantially and is worth avoiding entirely with hypertension.

Caffeine deserves a nuance: it raises blood pressure briefly (which is why you skip it before measuring), but moderate habitual coffee drinking is not linked to long-term hypertension.

Prove it's working

Diet changes show up in your readings over 2–8 weeks — but only if you measure. Track a baseline week before changing anything, then keep measuring a few mornings and evenings a week as you adjust. Comparing monthly averages against the blood pressure chart turns "I think it's helping" into a number you can see — and a chart your doctor will actually want to look at.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best drink for high blood pressure?

Water, first and always. Beyond that, beet juice has the strongest short-term trial evidence, and low-fat milk fits the DASH pattern. Limit sugary drinks, energy drinks, and alcohol.

How much sodium should I eat per day?

Under 2,300 mg/day is the general limit; around 1,500 mg is the ideal target for most adults with high blood pressure. Check labels — most sodium comes from processed food, not your salt shaker.

Does coffee raise blood pressure?

Temporarily, yes — for 30 minutes or more after a cup, which is why you should skip caffeine before measuring. Habitual moderate coffee drinking, however, is not associated with long-term hypertension in most studies.

How long until diet changes lower my blood pressure?

In the DASH trials, effects appeared within two weeks and grew over about eight. Track your weekly averages — see how to measure at home — so the change is visible rather than guessed.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Food changes complement, but never replace, prescribed medication — talk to your doctor before major dietary changes, especially if you have kidney disease (potassium) or take blood thinners. If a reading is higher than 180/120 mmHg and you have symptoms, seek emergency care immediately.
