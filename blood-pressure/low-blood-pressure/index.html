Low Blood Pressure (Hypotension): Causes, Symptoms, and When to Worry

Last updated: July 2026

Low blood pressure — hypotension — is generally defined as a reading below 90/60 mmHg. Unlike high blood pressure, low readings are often perfectly healthy: many fit people, and many younger women, live their whole lives around 90/60 and feel great. Low blood pressure becomes a problem when it causes symptoms, or when it drops suddenly.

Here's how to tell the harmless kind from the kind that needs attention.

Symptoms of low blood pressure

When blood pressure is too low for you, the brain is the first organ to complain. Typical symptoms:

  • Dizziness or light-headedness, especially on standing up
  • Blurred or narrowing vision ("graying out")
  • Fainting (syncope) or feeling about to faint
  • Fatigue, poor concentration, feeling "foggy"
  • Nausea, clammy or pale skin

The pattern matters as much as the number. A steady 88/58 with no symptoms is a very different situation from a drop of 20+ mmHg every time you stand — the latter is called orthostatic (postural) hypotension and is worth investigating, particularly in older adults, where it raises fall risk.

Common causes

Most low readings trace back to one of these:

  • Dehydration — the most common everyday cause; heat, exercise, illness, and simply not drinking enough all shrink blood volume.
  • Medications — blood pressure drugs (especially if the dose overshoots), diuretics, some antidepressants, Parkinson's drugs, and erectile dysfunction medicines.
  • Standing up quickly — gravity pools blood in the legs; ageing reflexes compensate more slowly.
  • After meals — blood diverts to digestion; postprandial dips are common in older adults.
  • Pregnancy — pressure typically runs lower, especially mid-pregnancy.
  • Heart, endocrine, and neurological conditions — arrhythmias, thyroid problems, adrenal insufficiency, and autonomic disorders can all lower pressure.
  • Sudden severe hypotension — from major blood loss, serious infection (sepsis), or severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) — is a medical emergency with shock symptoms: confusion, rapid weak pulse, cold clammy skin.

When to see a doctor

Make an appointment if:

  • You faint, or you repeatedly feel faint on standing
  • Symptoms are new, worsening, or interfering with daily life
  • Low readings started after a medication change — bring your readings log, as a dose adjustment is often the fix
  • Low blood pressure comes with chest pain, breathlessness, an irregular heartbeat, or black/tarry stools

Seek emergency care for signs of shock: cold clammy skin, rapid shallow breathing, a weak fast pulse, confusion.

If you're symptom-free at 88/56, mention it at your next routine visit — it's very likely just your normal. Knowing your baseline is what makes changes meaningful; home tracking a few times a week builds that baseline.

Self-care that helps

For everyday, symptom-level hypotension, doctors commonly suggest:

  • Drink more fluids, especially in heat and around exercise.
  • Stand up in stages — sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before rising; tense your leg muscles as you stand.
  • Eat smaller, more frequent meals if you dip after eating; consider less alcohol, which dilates vessels.
  • Compression stockings can help with pooling in the legs.
  • Salt — unlike in hypertension, a doctor may deliberately suggest more salt for symptomatic hypotension. Don't self-prescribe this if you have heart or kidney conditions; ask first.

Track readings alongside symptoms — noting when the dizziness happens and what the monitor said — so patterns like post-meal dips or a medication timing effect become visible. That's precisely the record a doctor needs to help you.

Frequently asked questions

Is 90/60 too low?

It's the conventional lower boundary. If you feel well at 90/60, it's usually just your normal — common in fit people and younger women. It matters when it comes with dizziness, fainting, or fatigue, or when it's a sudden change from your usual readings.

Why do I get dizzy when I stand up?

Standing pools blood in your legs, and if your circulation compensates slowly, brain blood flow briefly dips — orthostatic hypotension. Occasional mild episodes are common; frequent ones, or any that cause fainting, should be checked, especially if you take blood pressure medication.

Can low blood pressure be dangerous?

Chronically low but symptom-free pressure is usually harmless. The dangers are falls from fainting, and *sudden* severe drops from bleeding, sepsis, or anaphylaxis — those come with shock symptoms and are emergencies.

What raises blood pressure quickly when I feel faint?

Sit or lie down immediately (raising your legs helps), drink water, and tense your leg and buttock muscles. Prevention works better than rescue: hydrate through the day and stand up gradually.
