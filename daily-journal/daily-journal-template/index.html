Daily Journal Template

Last updated: August 2026

A template is the cheapest fix for the blank page. Instead of deciding what to write, you fill in fields — and a ninety-second entry still happens on the night you nearly skipped.

Five layouts follow, copyable into a notebook, a note, or a journal app. Use one for two weeks, then delete the lines that don't earn their place — a template you edited beats one you inherited.

What makes a template work

Most templates online are beautiful, have eleven fields, and get used four times. Three rules keep one alive:

  • Four to six fields, maximum. Every field is a nightly tax; anything you skip twice, delete.
  • Every field is a question, not a topic. "Reflection" produces a stare; "what went better than expected?" produces a sentence.
  • Include one open field. Fully structured entries become form-filling; the thing you didn't plan to write needs somewhere to go.

Decide once what the template is for — a memory log, a stress valve and a planning tool look different on the page. How to start journaling walks through the choice.

Template 1 — the five-minute daily

The default: balanced between recording and reflecting, short enough to survive a bad night.

FieldWhat to writeTime
Date & moodDate plus one or two words for how the day felt10 sec
What happenedThree sentences, facts only, no interpretation1 min
How I felt about itThe feeling, named as precisely as you can1 min
One good thingSomething specific that went right, however small30 sec
On my mindOpen field — anything still circling2 min

Filled in:

"Tue 12 Aug — flat but okay. Finished the handover doc, long call with Sam. Relieved about the doc, deflated that nobody noticed. One good thing: the walk back. On my mind: I want it acknowledged more than I want to admit."

Ninety seconds, and the last line is the one worth having.

Template 2 — the evening reflection

For closing the day; use it if you journal to sleep better.

  1. Today in one sentence.
  2. What went better than expected? Trains attention off the day's worst moment.
  3. What drained me? Over a month, the most informative field here.
  4. What am I still carrying? Everything unfinished, dumped out.
  5. Does it need to come to bed with me? Park each item, or write the next action and when.
  6. One thing I'm glad about today. Specific — "the way Dad laughed at his own joke," not "my family."

Steps four and five are the working parts: an open loop keeps you awake because it's stored nowhere else, and a written next action closes it well enough for sleep. If gratitude pulls you, keeping a gratitude journal covers keeping it fresh.

Template 3 — the weekly review

Once a week, same day, ten to fifteen minutes. The highest-value template here by a distance: daily entries record; the review is where you read your own life. Skip everything else before this.

SectionPrompt
RereadRead this week's entries first. Don't write anything yet.
PatternWhat came up more than once? What was I circling?
WinsThree things that went right, including ones nobody would congratulate me for.
DrainsWhere did my energy actually go? Was that the plan?
UnfinishedWhat's still open? What will honestly stay open?
Next weekOne thing to do more of, one less, one commitment with a day attached.

People skip the first row, and it's why the review works: rereading a week surfaces patterns no single entry contains — the visible payback a durable journaling habit runs on.

Templates 4 and 5 — the one-line log and the hard day

The one-line log

For busy stretches and travel weeks. Format: date — one sentence.

  • 12 Aug — First day back, inbox survivable, slept badly.
  • 13 Aug — Sam's news at lunch. Still processing it.

It looks trivial and isn't: a year of one-liners is a real record, costs fifteen seconds, and carries the habit through weeks that would break it. Make it the official fallback, not "skip tonight."

The hard day

For the days something has actually gone wrong.

  1. What happened, in plain order, no interpretation.
  2. What I'm feeling, named as precisely as I can.
  3. What I'm afraid this means. Then: fact, or fear?
  4. What's mine to fix and what isn't. Two lists.
  5. What I'd say to a friend in this exact situation.
  6. What I need right now — sleep, food, a conversation, a walk.

Step one separates what happened from what you concluded — usually tangled. If the same hard thing keeps returning, journaling for anxiety and stress covers the line between processing and rumination.

Frequently asked questions

How many fields should a daily journal template have?

Four to six. Every field is a small nightly tax, and templates with ten fields get abandoned inside a month. Keep at least one open field for whatever you didn't plan to write — that's usually where the useful entries come from.

Should I use the same template every day?

Mostly yes — the point of a template is that you stop deciding. Keep one daily layout, plus a one-line fallback for busy stretches and a separate template for hard days. Review the fields monthly and delete anything you keep skipping.

Do templates make journaling feel mechanical?

They can, if every field is closed. The fix is the open field at the end: structure gets you started, then you follow whatever shows up. If entries still feel like form-filling, swap two fields for a prompt that changes weekly.

Is a weekly review worth the extra time?

It's the highest-value item on this page. Daily entries record; the weekly review is where you read them back and see patterns no single entry contains. If you only have time for one thing, do the review and let some daily entries go.

Can I use these templates on paper?

Yes — every layout here was written to be copyable by hand. On paper, write the headings on the next page before bed so the page is ready when you sit down. The only thing paper can't do is recopy the template for you and let you search the fields later.

A note on wellbeing

Journaling is a wellbeing practice, not a treatment. A template can make difficult feelings easier to approach, but writing doesn't replace professional mental-health support. If journaling consistently surfaces distress — persistent low mood, anxiety that doesn't ease, or thoughts of harming yourself — talk to a doctor or therapist. If you are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line in your country.
