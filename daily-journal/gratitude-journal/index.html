How to Keep a Gratitude Journal

Last updated: August 2026

A gratitude journal is a record of the things you're glad about — the most researched, most recommended, and most quietly abandoned journaling practice there is. Studies consistently find it improves mood and sleep more than neutral writing. The catch: the effect depends almost entirely on how you do it.

Most die the same death — the same three bullets every night until the page becomes a chore. Here's what the practice actually asks, how often to write, fifteen prompts, and what to do when nothing comes.

What a gratitude journal is — and isn't

On a schedule, write down specific good things and — crucially — why they were good. The second half separates it from a shopping list of nice nouns.

It isn't forced positivity — the honest version has room for "today was mostly grim, but my sister called at the right moment." And it isn't a mood tracker: a rating records the day; writing about one good moment makes you re-live it. The wider benefits of journaling apply here too.

Specificity beats "three things"

"List three things" is where the power leaks out: "my family, my health, my job" is true every day, so your brain stops noticing — habituation. The fix is depth: three to five sentences about one thing — what happened, who was involved, why it mattered to you.

Instead ofTry
My friendsSam messaged out of nowhere to ask how the interview went. He remembered the date. I hadn't told anyone else.
Good coffeeTwenty minutes on the back step with a coffee before anyone else was up. The street was completely silent. I'd forgotten mornings could be that quiet.
My healthWalked up the hill without stopping halfway. Six months ago I stopped twice. My legs are quietly getting on with it.
My jobPriya took the difficult call so I didn't have to. Nobody asked her to and she never mentioned it afterwards.

The right column is people doing things, or a moment with a time and a place — concrete scenes don't habituate.

How often should you write?

Daily is the default advice, but research repeatedly finds once or twice a week works as well. Frequency isn't the active ingredient; attention is.

  • Daily if you enjoy it — switch to a prompt when entries start repeating.
  • Two or three times a week if daily feels like homework.
  • Long and less often if you warm up slowly.

Anchor it to something that already happens — teeth brushed, house quiet. More in how to build a journaling habit.

Fifteen gratitude prompts

Pick one at random — the randomness is doing real work.

People

  • Who made your day easier today, and did they know they were doing it?
  • Who would notice first if you disappeared for a week?
  • Describe a small kindness you received and never acknowledged.

Ordinary days

  • What went right today that you'd have panicked about if it had gone wrong?
  • What did you eat today that you actually enjoyed?
  • What worked today without you having to think about it?

Body and senses

  • What did your body let you do today?
  • Describe the best thing you heard today — a voice, music, silence.
  • What did you smell today that took you somewhere else?

Difficulty

  • What hard thing are you glad you went through, and what did it leave you with?
  • Which problem you had last year is no longer a problem?
  • Who stayed when things were difficult?
  • What are you grateful not to be dealing with right now?

The awkward ones

  • What are you grateful for that you'd feel embarrassed admitting?
  • Who do you owe an apology to, and what do you appreciate about them anyway?

When it goes stale — or feels dishonest

Every long-running gratitude journal hits the wall. What rescues one:

  • Subtract instead of add. Imagine the thing had never happened — the job you didn't get, the friend you nearly didn't meet. Removal restores weight faster than listing.
  • Write it as a letter to the person it's about, sent or not — impossible on autopilot.
  • Shrink the unit. The bus was on time; the cat sat on you. Small and true beats large and performative.
  • Skip a week deliberately. A planned pause is not a broken streak.
  • Reread. Monthly, read last year's entries — the payoff almost nobody collects.

If writing consistently drops your mood, that's a finding, not a failure — some people do better with expressive writing about what's actually bothering them.

Frequently asked questions

Should I write in my gratitude journal every day?

Not necessarily. Research consistently finds that once or twice a week works at least as well as daily — write too often and your brain gets the same material repeatedly and stops noticing. Write daily if you enjoy it, but switch to a prompt the moment entries start repeating. See how to build a journaling habit for making either schedule stick.

What should I write if I can't think of anything?

Shrink the unit until something is true: the bus came, the coffee was hot, nobody needed anything from you for ten minutes. Small and specific beats large and vague. If that fails, use a prompt — "what went right today that I'd have panicked about if it had gone wrong?" almost always produces something.

Is a gratitude journal the same as a daily journal?

No — it's one section of one. A gratitude journal has a single focus; a daily journal records whatever the day held. Many people run both in the same place, with a short gratitude block at the end of a normal entry. A daily journal template makes that easy to repeat.

Morning or evening?

Evening suits most people: the day's material is already there, and the practice winds you down before sleep. Morning works if you'd rather set the tone than review it. Consistency of *time* matters far more than which time you pick.

Does it still work if I write on my phone?

Yes. What matters is attention and specificity, not the medium. A phone is with you when the good thing actually happens, and it makes rereading old entries possible — where much of the long-term value lives. See digital vs paper journaling for the full trade-off.
