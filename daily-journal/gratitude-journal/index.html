How to Keep a Gratitude Journal

Last updated: August 2026

A gratitude journal is a record of the things you're glad about. It is the most researched, most recommended and most quietly abandoned journaling practice there is — studies consistently find that people who write regularly about what they appreciate report better mood and sleep than people who write about neutral daily events. The catch is that the effect depends almost entirely on how you do it.

Most gratitude journals die the same death: three bullet points before bed, roughly the same three every night, until the page becomes a small nightly chore you quietly resent. This guide covers what the practice actually asks of you, how often to write, more than twenty prompts to keep it from calcifying, and what to do on the evenings when nothing comes to mind.

What a gratitude journal is — and isn't

The practice is simple to describe: on a regular schedule, you write down specific good things and, crucially, why they were good. That second half is what separates a gratitude journal from a shopping list of nice nouns.

What it isn't: it isn't forced positivity, and it isn't an instruction to be pleased about things that are genuinely bad. A gratitude journal that requires you to pretend a hard week was secretly fine will make you feel worse, not better — and you'll stop writing within two weeks. The honest version has room for "today was mostly grim, but my sister called at exactly the right moment." Both halves of that sentence are true, and writing both is what keeps the habit credible to yourself.

It also isn't a mood tracker. Rating your day 4/10 records what happened to you; writing about a specific good moment makes you re-live it, which is where the benefit is thought to come from. The wider benefits of journaling apply here too, but gratitude writing has its own particular ask: attention to the good, in detail, on purpose.

Specificity beats "three things"

The standard advice — list three things you're grateful for — is a starting scaffold that most people never move past. And listing is exactly where the practice loses its power. "My family, my health, my job" is true on Monday and true on Friday, so by Friday your brain has stopped paying attention. The word for this is habituation, and it is the biggest reason gratitude journals go flat.

The fix is depth, not breadth. One entry written properly does more than five bullets. Aim for three to five sentences about one thing: what happened, who was involved, what you noticed, and why it mattered to you specifically. If you want a rough target, write until you have said something you didn't already know when you picked up the pen.

Instead ofTry
My friendsSam messaged out of nowhere to ask how the interview went. He remembered the date. I hadn't told anyone else.
Good coffeeTwenty minutes on the back step with a coffee before anyone else was up. The street was completely silent. I'd forgotten mornings could be that quiet.

My jobPriya took the difficult call so I didn't have to. Nobody asked her to and she never mentioned it afterwards.

Notice that the right-hand column is almost always about people doing things, or a moment with a time and a place attached. Abstract categories habituate fast; concrete scenes don't.

How often should you write?

Daily is the default recommendation, but it is not obviously the best one. Research on gratitude interventions has repeatedly found that writing once or twice a week can produce as much benefit as writing every day — and sometimes more, because a weekly entry has fresh material while a nightly one starts scraping the barrel. Frequency isn't the active ingredient. Attention is.

A practical way to decide:

  • Write daily if you enjoy it, and treat repetition as a warning sign rather than a failure. The day you catch yourself writing the same entry as yesterday, switch to a prompt.
  • Write two or three times a week if daily feels like homework. Sunday evening and Wednesday evening is a perfectly good gratitude practice.
  • Write long, less often if you're the sort of person who warms up slowly. One properly explored entry a week beats seven perfunctory ones.

Whatever you choose, anchor it to something that already happens — after you brush your teeth, on the train, once the house is quiet. That's the mechanism behind every journaling habit that survives its first month; there's more on making it stick in how to build a journaling habit.

20+ gratitude prompts

Prompts exist to stop you writing the same entry twice. Pick one at random rather than working down the list in order — the randomness is doing real work.

People

  • Who made your day easier today, and did they know they were doing it?
  • Write about someone who taught you something you still use.
  • Which relationship in your life takes the least effort? Why?
  • Who would notice first if you disappeared for a week?
  • Describe a small kindness you received and never acknowledged.

Ordinary days

  • What went right today that you'd have panicked about if it had gone wrong?
  • Name something in your home you'd immediately replace if it broke.
  • What did you eat today that you actually enjoyed?
  • Which part of your routine would you miss most if it vanished?
  • What worked today without you having to think about it?

Body and senses

  • What did your body let you do today?
  • Describe the best thing you heard today — a voice, music, silence.
  • Where were you warm, comfortable, or unhurried?
  • What did you smell today that took you somewhere else?

Difficulty

  • What hard thing are you glad you went through, and what did it leave you with?
  • Which problem you had last year is no longer a problem?
  • Who stayed when things were difficult?
  • What are you grateful not to be dealing with right now?

Past and future

  • What decision from five years ago are you still benefiting from?
  • Write a thank-you to a past version of yourself.
  • What are you looking forward to, and who helped make it possible?
  • Which thing you once wanted badly do you now have?

The awkward ones

  • What are you grateful for that you'd feel embarrassed admitting?
  • What did you almost lose this year?
  • Who do you owe an apology to, and what do you appreciate about them anyway?

When it goes stale — or feels dishonest

Every long-running gratitude journal hits the wall. Here's what actually rescues one:

  • Subtract instead of add. Rather than asking what you're grateful for, imagine the thing had never happened — the job you didn't get, the friend you nearly didn't meet. Mentally removing something restores its weight far faster than listing it again.
  • Write it as a letter. Address an entry to the person it's about, whether or not you ever send it. Gratitude letters are one of the more reliably effective versions of the practice, and they're very hard to do on autopilot.
  • Shrink the unit. On bad days, drop the standard to something almost trivially small and specific: the bus was on time; the cat sat on you. Small and true beats large and performative.
  • Skip a week deliberately. A planned pause is not a broken streak, and coming back after seven days off usually produces a better entry than grinding through.
  • Reread. Once a month, read what you wrote a year ago. This is the payoff the practice quietly builds toward, and almost nobody collects it.

One honest caveat: if sitting down to write consistently drops your mood rather than lifting it, gratitude journaling may not be the right practice for you right now — that's a legitimate finding, not a failure. Some people do better with expressive writing about what's actually bothering them.

Notebook, notes app, or journal app: an honest look

A gratitude journal needs almost nothing. A $3 notebook by the bed is a complete implementation of everything above, and the physical ritual — pen, paper, lamp — genuinely helps some people show up. Nobody should be talked out of that.

What paper costs you shows up in year two. You can't search it, so the entry about your grandmother is somewhere in a box. It isn't with you when the good moment happens, so it gets written from memory at 11pm. And rereading — the best thing you can do with old gratitude entries — means finding the right notebook and the right page, which in practice means you don't.

Frequently asked questions

Should I write in my gratitude journal every day?

Not necessarily. Research on gratitude writing consistently finds that once or twice a week works at least as well as daily — writing too often gives your brain the same material repeatedly, and it stops noticing. Write daily if you enjoy it, but switch to a prompt the moment entries start repeating. See how to build a journaling habit for making either schedule stick.

What should I write if I can't think of anything?

Shrink the unit until something is true: the bus came, the coffee was hot, nobody needed anything from you for ten minutes. Small and specific beats large and vague. If that still fails, use a prompt — asking "what went right today that I'd have panicked about if it had gone wrong?" almost always produces something.

Is a gratitude journal the same as a daily journal?

No — it's one section of one. A gratitude journal has a single focus; a daily journal records whatever the day held. Many people run both in the same place, with a short gratitude block at the end of a normal entry. A daily journal template makes that easy to repeat.

Morning or evening?

Evening suits most people, because the day's material is already there and the practice winds you down before sleep. Morning works if you'd rather set the tone than review it — you'll be writing about yesterday, which is fine. Consistency of *time* matters far more than which time you pick.

Does it still work if I write on my phone?

Yes. What matters is attention and specificity, not the medium. A phone is with you when the good thing actually happens, which is a real advantage — and it makes rereading old entries possible, which is where a lot of the long-term value lives.
