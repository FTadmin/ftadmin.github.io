How to Start Journaling

Last updated: August 2026

Starting a journal is easy. Entries two and three are where people stall — not because writing is hard, but because they never decided what a normal entry looks like, so every session reopens the same question: what am I supposed to say?

This guide closes it: a definition of "enough" that takes three minutes, a first week with the decisions already made, and the boring, fixable reasons journals die in week two.

First, decide what your journal is for

"Journal" covers five different habits. Pick one before your first entry — you can change later, but today's entry needs a job.

  • To think clearly. You write to finish a thought that's been circling. Messy and argumentative is correct.
  • To remember. What happened, who was there, what it felt like. Short, factual, weirdly valuable five years on.
  • To decompress. You put down the thing you can't say out loud yet — the version with the strongest evidence behind it (see the benefits of journaling).
  • To notice patterns. Sleep, mood, the same argument every third Sunday. Patterns need dated, searchable entries.
  • To make things. Fragments, lines, half-ideas. Morning pages live here.

Beginners quietly try all five at once, in nice handwriting — that's why the blank page feels heavy. One purpose, one entry.

How little is enough

Less than you think. A complete entry is three sentences: one thing that happened, one thing you felt about it, one thing to remember or do next. A whole entry, and it counts:

"Presentation went fine, better than I expected. Still felt shaky for an hour afterwards, which is annoying because it went fine. Note: the shakiness isn't information about how it went."

Ninety seconds. On a flat day, one line — "Nothing much. Tired. Bed early." — is still a real entry; a run of dull entries is data about your life, not a failure of writing. Rather fill in fields? Start from a daily journal template.

Two rules from day one: never reread while writing — editing mid-entry turns journaling into homework — and write badly on purpose for two weeks. Nobody is reading this; an imagined audience turns a journal into a performance.

Your first week, planned for you

The best predictor of whether journaling sticks is whether you had to decide anything. So don't — each day has its question, none over five minutes.

Day 7 matters most: rereading your own week is when journaling starts paying you back. After that, keep a stack of prompts so a flat day never becomes a skipped day.

Why people quit in week two

Week one runs on novelty; week two runs on nothing. The reasons people quit are remarkably consistent:

  1. The entries got long. A twenty-minute journal loses to sleep every time. Shrink the entry before you shrink the habit.
  2. A missed day broke the streak. This ends more journals than boredom. A missed day is not a verdict — write the next one, don't backfill, don't apologize.
  3. Wrong hour. Hating journaling at 11pm means the hour is wrong, not you. Try right after coffee, the train, the parked car.
  4. Nothing happened. Flat days feel unwritable, get skipped, and skipping becomes the habit. On an empty day, answer a question instead.
  5. It felt like it was for someone else. Careful handwriting, tidy conclusions. Break that voice with one deliberately ugly entry.
  6. No cue. Intention is not a trigger — see how to build a journaling habit for cue design and the two-minute version.

Not on the list: being a bad writer. Nobody quits for that.

Frequently asked questions

What should I write in my first journal entry?

Write why you opened the journal at all, and what you'd like to be different in a month. It's the one entry you'll be glad to have later, and it needs no material beyond what's in your head. Too big? Three sentences about yesterday instead.

How long should a journal entry be?

Three sentences is a complete entry. Aim for three to five minutes — long enough to finish a thought, short enough that a tired evening doesn't beat it. Length is the most common reason a new journal collapses; when in doubt, write less and keep going.

Do I have to journal every day?

No. Daily is the easiest rhythm to remember, not a requirement — three or four honest entries a week beats seven dutiful ones. What matters is that a missed day doesn't end the habit. See how to build a journaling habit for what to do after a gap.

What if nothing happened today?

Then write that. "Nothing much, tired, bed early" is a real entry, and a run of them tells you something about the month. On flat days, answer a question instead — keep a few journal prompts on hand for exactly this.

Should I write by hand or on my phone?

Whichever you'll actually do. Handwriting is slower in a way that can help you think; a phone is with you when the thought arrives and can be searched years later. If you've abandoned paper journals before, the problem was probably logistics — the part an app fixes.

A note on wellbeing

Journaling is a wellbeing practice, not a treatment. It supports reflection but doesn't replace professional mental-health care. If writing consistently surfaces distress — persistent low mood, anxiety that doesn't ease, or thoughts of harming yourself — talk to a doctor or therapist. If you are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line in your country.
