Sleep Hygiene: Habits That Work

Last updated: August 2026

Sleep hygiene is the unglamorous set of daily habits that make good sleep likely. It's not a cure — telling someone with chronic insomnia to "try a cooler room" is unhelpful — but for the far larger group whose sleep is mediocre, the basics are often the whole fix.

Most lists give twenty items equal weight. They don't deserve it. Here's the checklist by daytime, evening and bedroom, then an honest ranking — so you fix the three that matter first.

Daytime habits

Good sleep is built during the day, via your body clock and sleep pressure — the drive that builds the longer you're awake.

  • Keep one wake time, every day, weekends included — it's the anchor; a two-hour weekend shift is jetlag that wrecks Sunday night.
  • Get bright light early — 10–20 minutes outdoors soon after waking. Overcast daylight still dwarfs indoor lighting; the strongest lever for an earlier clock.
  • Exercise, but not right before bed — it improves quality and shortens onset; just avoid hard sessions in the last 2–3 hours. 8 pm beats never.
  • Cut caffeine after early afternoon — five-hour half-life: a 3 pm coffee is still active at midnight, trimming deep sleep. Stop 8–10 hours out.
  • Naps: 20–30 minutes, before mid-afternoon — longer or later eats tonight's sleep pressure.

Evening habits

The last few hours: lower arousal, let core temperature fall — the two things sleep onset depends on.

  • A 30–60 minute wind-down, same sequence most nights — predictability turns it into a cue.
  • Dim the lights — lamps, not overheads, for the last hour; better for melatonin than any screen filter.
  • Stop stimulating content, not just blue light — work email, news, a gripping episode. Arousal outlasts screen brightness; see how to fall asleep faster.
  • Warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed — the rebound cooling speeds sleep onset.
  • Skip the nightcap. Alcohol brings sleep faster, then suppresses REM and fragments the second half — one good hour for three bad ones.
  • Park tomorrow on paper — five minutes of writing stops the list circulating after lights out.

Bedroom habits and environment

The room's job: boring, dark, cool, quiet — and meaning one thing to your brain.

  • Cool: 16–19 °C (60–67 °F). Err cooler, add a blanket.
  • Dark: blackout curtains or an eye mask; cover standby LEDs.
  • Quiet: earplugs or steady background noise, masking the intermittent sounds that actually wake you.
  • Bed is for sleep and sex only — no working, scrolling or eating. That's what makes the bed feel sleepy.
  • Don't lie awake in it. After about 20 minutes: up, dim light, something dull, back when sleepy — stimulus control, the heart of CBT-I.
  • Turn the clock away — minute-watching is pure arousal.

Change the top three before anything else — the ranking reflects effect size and reach, not listicle frequency.

For chronic insomnia, hygiene alone is not the treatment — guidelines put CBT-I first. And if you sleep nine hours and wake exhausted, or a partner reports snoring and breathing pauses, no routine fixes that — see sleep apnea signs and talk to a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

What is sleep hygiene, exactly?

The daily habits and conditions that make good sleep likely — wake time, light exposure, caffeine and alcohol timing, the wind-down, the bedroom itself. It's preventive maintenance, not a treatment: it makes ordinary sleep better, but it isn't sufficient for chronic insomnia or an untreated sleep disorder.

How long before better sleep hygiene works?

Give any single change one to two weeks. Sleep is noisy night to night, so one bad night after a change proves nothing. Shifting your body clock — a new wake time, morning light — typically takes a few days to a week to feel normal.

Is it bad to read or watch TV in bed?

It depends on whether it ends in sleep. A few pages of something undemanding that leads to lights-out is fine; an hour of screen time because you aren't sleepy trains your brain that the bed is for being awake. If that's the pattern, do it in a chair and move to bed when your eyes start closing.

Does sleep hygiene cure insomnia?

No, and it's worth being clear about that. For chronic insomnia, clinical guidelines put CBT-I first — hygiene is one component of it, not a substitute. See insomnia: causes and what helps, and talk to your doctor if it has lasted more than a few weeks.

What's the best room temperature for sleep?

Around 16–19 °C (60–67 °F) for most people. Falling asleep depends on your core temperature dropping, so a warm room delays it no matter how tired you are. If in doubt, err cooler and add a blanket.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Sleep hygiene supports, but does not replace, professional care — always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider. See a clinician if poor sleep persists for more than a few weeks despite good habits, if it affects your daytime functioning or mood, if you fall asleep unintentionally during the day, or if a partner reports loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in your breathing.
