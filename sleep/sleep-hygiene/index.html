Sleep Hygiene: Habits That Work

Last updated: August 2026

Sleep hygiene is the unglamorous set of daily habits that make good sleep likely. It's not a cure — telling someone with chronic insomnia to "try a cooler room" is unhelpful — but for the far larger group of people whose sleep is simply mediocre, getting the basics right is often the whole fix.

The problem with most sleep hygiene lists is that they present twenty items as if each carries equal weight. They don't. Below is the full checklist, grouped by daytime, evening and bedroom, followed by an honest ranking of which few items actually do the heavy lifting — so you can start with those rather than trying to overhaul your entire life on a Sunday night.

Daytime habits

Good sleep is built during the day. These set your body clock and your sleep pressure — the drive to sleep that accumulates the longer you're awake.

  • Keep one wake time, every day. Including weekends. This is the anchor everything else hangs on; a two-hour weekend shift produces a jetlag-like swing that wrecks Sunday night.
  • Get bright light early. 10–20 minutes outdoors within an hour or two of waking. Outdoor light on an overcast day still dwarfs indoor lighting, and it's the strongest tool you have for shifting your clock earlier.
  • Exercise, but not right before bed. Regular activity reliably improves sleep quality and shortens the time it takes to drop off. Hard sessions in the last 2–3 hours can delay onset by raising core temperature — morning or afternoon is ideal, but exercising at 8 pm beats not exercising.
  • Cut caffeine after early afternoon. Its half-life is around five hours, so a 3 pm coffee is still measurably active at midnight and reduces deep sleep even if you fall asleep fine. Aim to stop 8–10 hours before bed.
  • Keep naps short and early. 20–30 minutes before mid-afternoon. Longer or later naps eat the sleep pressure you need for tonight.
  • Eat and drink on a reasonably regular schedule — meal timing is a secondary body-clock cue.

Evening habits

The last two to three hours are about lowering arousal and letting core body temperature fall — the two things sleep onset depends on.

  • Build a wind-down routine of 30–60 minutes. Same sequence most nights, nothing demanding. The predictability is the point: it becomes a cue rather than a chore.
  • Dim the lights. Lamps instead of overheads for the last hour. This does more for melatonin than any screen filter.
  • Stop stimulating content, not just blue light. Work email, news, a gripping episode — arousal keeps you awake more reliably than screen brightness does. See how to fall asleep faster for the detail.
  • Warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed. The rebound cooling afterward speeds sleep onset — one of the better-supported non-drug interventions.
  • Skip the nightcap. Alcohol gets you to sleep faster and then suppresses REM and fragments the second half of the night. It's the classic trade of one good hour for three bad ones.
  • Finish heavy meals 2–3 hours before bed, and taper fluids so you're not up at 3 am.
  • Park tomorrow on paper. Five minutes writing down what's on your mind, before you get into bed, stops the same list circulating once the lights are off.

Bedroom habits and environment

The room's job is to be boring, dark, cool and quiet — and to mean one thing to your brain.

  • Cool: roughly 16–19 °C (60–67 °F). Err cooler and add a blanket.
  • Dark: blackout curtains or an eye mask, and cover standby LEDs. Light in the second half of the night can end sleep earlier than you'd like.
  • Quiet: earplugs or steady background noise. Steady sound masks the intermittent sound that actually wakes you.
  • Bed is for sleep and sex only. No working, scrolling or eating in it. This association is what makes the bed feel sleepy rather than activating.
  • Don't lie awake in it. Awake after about 20 minutes? Get up, dim light, something dull, return when sleepy. Repeat as needed — this is the stimulus-control technique at the heart of CBT-I.
  • Turn the clock away. Watching the minutes is pure arousal with no upside.
  • Mattress and pillow you find comfortable — no brand or firmness is objectively correct, and expensive doesn't mean better for you.

Which items actually matter most

If you change nothing else, change the top three. The ranking below reflects the strength of the effect and how many people it applies to — not how often each item appears on listicles.

And the honest caveat: for chronic insomnia, hygiene alone is not the treatment — clinical guidelines put CBT-I first. If you're sleeping nine hours and still exhausted, or a partner has mentioned snoring and pauses in your breathing, no bedtime routine will fix that either; see sleep apnea signs and talk to a doctor.

Notebook vs app: which habit is yours?

The checklist above is generic by necessity. Your actual problem is probably one or two specific items on it, and the way to find out which is embarrassingly simple: change one thing at a time and write down what happened. Give each change a week — sleep is noisy, and a single night proves nothing either way.

A notebook by the bed does this perfectly. Write down your bedtime, your wake time, a quality rating out of five, and one line about the evening. If you'll actually keep it, that's all you need — and there is a real argument for paper, since it doesn't involve picking up a phone at bedtime.

Where paper loses is the review. Answering "were the weeks I stopped drinking coffee after 2 pm actually better?" means adding up scribbled times across fourteen pages, so almost nobody does it, and the diary becomes a record nobody reads.

Frequently asked questions

What is sleep hygiene, exactly?

The set of daily habits and environmental conditions that make good sleep likely — wake time, light exposure, caffeine and alcohol timing, the wind-down routine, and the bedroom itself. It's preventive maintenance, not a treatment: it makes ordinary sleep better and it isn't sufficient on its own for chronic insomnia or an untreated sleep disorder.

How long before better sleep hygiene works?

Give any single change one to two weeks. Sleep is noisy night to night, so one bad night after a change proves nothing. Shifting your body clock — a new wake time, morning light — typically takes a few days to a week to feel normal.

Is it bad to read or watch TV in bed?

It depends on whether it ends in sleep. A few pages of something undemanding that leads to lights-out is fine; an hour of screen time because you aren't sleepy trains your brain that the bed is for being awake. If that's the pattern, do it in a chair and move to bed when your eyes start closing.

Does sleep hygiene cure insomnia?

No, and it's worth being clear about that. For chronic insomnia, clinical guidelines put CBT-I first — hygiene is one component of it, not a substitute. See insomnia: causes and what helps, and talk to your doctor if it has been going on for more than a few weeks.

What's the best room temperature for sleep?

Around 16–19 °C (60–67 °F) for most people. Falling asleep depends on your core temperature dropping, so a warm room delays onset no matter how tired you are. If in doubt, err cooler and add a blanket.
