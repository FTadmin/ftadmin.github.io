Sleep Diary Template

Last updated: August 2026

A sleep diary is a daily record of when you slept, how you slept, and how the following day went. It takes about two minutes each morning, and it is the single most useful thing you can bring to a doctor about your sleep — because memory is a terrible witness. Lying awake distorts time, and almost everyone misjudges how long they actually slept.

Here's a template you can copy into a notebook or spreadsheet, exactly what to fill in, why clinicians ask for two weeks, and how a diary gets used alongside a sleep study.

The template

One row per night, filled in the next morning. This layout covers everything a clinician needs and nothing they don't:

If that's more columns than you'll keep up with, cut it down — a five-column diary you complete for fourteen days beats an eleven-column one you abandon on day four. The non-negotiables are lights out, final wake time, and a quality rating.

What to record each morning

Fill it in within the first half hour of getting up, before the night blurs.

  • To bed vs lights out — two different times, and the gap is diagnostic on its own. Forty minutes of scrolling in bed every night is a finding.
  • Asleep by (estimate) — a guess is fine; clinicians expect one. The two-week pattern is what counts.
  • Wake-ups — how many, and roughly how long awake in total. "3 (~40 min)" is enough.
  • Final wake and out of bed — also two times. A long gap between waking and rising is one of the habits that keeps insomnia alive; see insomnia causes.
  • Time slept — time in bed minus time awake in it. This is the number that gets averaged.
  • Quality 1–5 — how restored you felt, not how long you slept. Subjective is the point.
  • Naps — length and time of day; long or late naps quietly cut the next night's sleep pressure.
  • Notes — where the answers hide: caffeine after midday, alcohol, exercise, medication, shifts, travel, stress, next-day sleepiness (a 1–5 works), plus symptoms like morning headache, dry mouth, snoring your partner mentioned, bathroom trips.

One rule: don't clock-watch. Checking the time repeatedly makes both your sleep and your diary worse. Turn the clock away, estimate in the morning — that is exactly how clinicians read it.

The two-week rule

Fourteen consecutive nights is the standard ask, for good reasons rather than a round number:

  • Single nights are noise. A pattern needs enough nights to separate signal from a stressful Tuesday.
  • It captures weekdays and weekends, twice. The weekday-weekend gap — social jetlag — is one of the most common, most fixable findings a diary produces.
  • It covers a full cycle of work rhythm, exercise, alcohol, travel, and for some people the menstrual cycle's second half.
  • It gives a baseline — a credible "before" for whatever you change next.

Include the bad nights. A diary edited to look respectable is worse than none, because it sends your clinician down the wrong path. Missed a morning? Leave the row blank — a gap is honest data, an invented row isn't.

At the end, work out three numbers: average time slept, average time in bed, and sleep efficiency (time asleep divided by time in bed, as a percentage). Above roughly 85% is generally considered healthy; much lower usually means too much time in bed for the sleep you're getting — exactly the pattern behavioral treatment targets.

How clinicians use a sleep diary

Sleep clinics routinely ask for one or two weeks of diary before and alongside testing, because it answers questions no single night in a lab can.

Before a sleep study, the diary establishes your normal. A study measures one or two nights of physiology; the diary supplies the context — that you average five and a half hours, or that your bedtime swings by three hours — and helps schedule the study sensibly, which matters a great deal for shift workers.

To tell conditions apart. The big possibilities look similar from the inside and different on paper: insufficient sleep shows normal quality but too few hours; a circadian rhythm disorder shows good sleep at the "wrong" clock times; insomnia shows long sleep-onset times or long awakenings despite plenty of opportunity. Repeated unexplained awakenings plus daytime sleepiness with normal hours raises the question of a breathing problem — see sleep apnea signs.

To run treatment. Behavioral treatment for insomnia is driven directly by diary numbers — prescribed time in bed is set from your average sleep time and widened as efficiency improves. On CPAP therapy, the diary sits next to the machine's usage data: the machine says how many hours it ran, the diary says how you slept and felt.

Bring the whole thing, not a summary. Your clinician wants the spread and the outliers, not just the average.

Paper works. This template by the bed, a pen, and two minutes each morning is a complete, clinically useful sleep diary. Frequently asked questions

How long should I keep a sleep diary?

Two consecutive weeks is the standard: it captures weekdays and weekends twice and lets a pattern separate itself from a couple of bad nights. One week is the minimum most clinicians will work from; longer is useful when tracking the effect of a change.

What if I don't know exactly when I fell asleep?

Estimate — that's what everyone does, and what clinicians expect. Deliberately don't watch the clock during the night: checking the time repeatedly makes both your sleep and your diary worse. Fill the row in from your best guess within half an hour of getting up.

Should I record bad nights?

Especially the bad nights. A tidied-up diary is worse than none, because it points your clinician at the wrong problem. If you miss a morning entirely, leave the row blank rather than reconstructing it days later.

What is sleep efficiency and what's a good number?

Time actually asleep divided by time in bed, as a percentage. Above roughly 85% is generally considered healthy. A much lower figure usually means you're spending more time in bed than you're sleeping — the exact pattern that behavioral treatment for insomnia is designed to correct.

Does a sleep diary replace a sleep study?

No — they answer different questions and clinics use both. A study measures your breathing, oxygen and brain activity for a night or two; a diary shows your habitual pattern across weeks. If a breathing problem is suspected, only a study can confirm it; see sleep apnea signs.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. A sleep diary supports, but never replaces, professional assessment — always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider, and never start, stop, or change a medication on your own. See a doctor promptly if you fall asleep uncontrollably during the day, if daytime sleepiness is affecting your driving, or if someone has seen you stop breathing, gasp, or choke in your sleep.
