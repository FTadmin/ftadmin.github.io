Insomnia: Causes and What Helps

Last updated: August 2026

Insomnia is trouble falling asleep, trouble staying asleep, or waking far too early — despite having the time and the opportunity to sleep — and paying for it the next day. It is the most common sleep complaint there is, and most of the time it has a cause you can name.

This guide covers what separates acute from chronic insomnia, the main categories of cause, and why the treatment with the strongest long-term evidence is a structured behavioral program rather than a prescription. If you mainly need help with tonight, start with how to fall asleep faster.

Acute vs chronic insomnia

Clinicians split insomnia by how long it has been going on, because the two call for very different responses.

  • Acute (short-term) insomnia lasts days to a few weeks and usually has an obvious trigger: a deadline, grief, illness, jet lag, a new baby, a noisy hotel room. It fades when the trigger does, and most people never need treatment for it.
  • Chronic insomnia is the clinical threshold — disturbed sleep at least three nights a week for three months or longer, together with a daytime consequence such as fatigue, irritability, poor concentration, or low mood. By this point the original trigger is often long gone.

The daytime half of that definition does a lot of work. Someone who sleeps six hours, wakes up clear-headed and functions well all day is a short sleeper, not an insomniac. Insomnia is the gap between the sleep you get and the sleep your body needs — see how much sleep you actually need for what that range looks like.

The other thing worth knowing early: almost everyone overestimates how long they lay awake. Lying in the dark distorts time badly, which is one reason a written record beats memory when you are trying to work out what is really happening.

What causes insomnia

Insomnia is a symptom with many possible drivers, and most people who have had it for months are dealing with more than one at a time. These are the categories clinicians work through:

Stress and life eventsWork pressure, grief, money worries, a move, a new babyA racing mind at lights-out; trouble falling asleep
Habits and environmentIrregular bed and wake times, screens in bed, a warm, bright or noisy bedroom, long late napsLight, broken sleep; hard to settle

That last row is the one most often missed. Untreated sleep apnea can look exactly like insomnia from the inside — you wake repeatedly and have no idea why — and no amount of sleep hygiene will fix it. If you snore heavily, wake gasping, or someone has watched you stop breathing, that gets investigated first.

Why insomnia outlives its trigger

The standard clinical model of chronic insomnia has three parts, and it explains the most frustrating thing about the condition: the cause that started it is usually not the cause keeping it going.

  1. Predisposing factors — the baseline you were born with or built up: a light-sleeping temperament, a tendency to worry, a family history of poor sleep.
  2. Precipitating factors — the event that tipped you over. Illness, a bereavement, a brutal work quarter, a newborn.
  3. Perpetuating factors — everything you sensibly started doing to cope, which quietly makes it permanent: going to bed two hours early to "catch up," lying in on weekends, napping, staying in bed awake, checking the clock, and monitoring your own sleepiness all evening.

Those coping habits are the real target. Spending nine hours in bed to get five hours of sleep dilutes your sleep drive and teaches your brain that bed is a place where you lie awake — so the bed itself starts to trigger alertness. That is why insomnia can persist for years after the divorce, the deadline or the illness that started it has been resolved.

It also explains why the fix is behavioral rather than pharmacological. You are not treating the original event; you are undoing the loop that formed around it.

CBT-I is the first-line treatment — not sleeping pills

For chronic insomnia, major clinical guidelines recommend cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) before medication. Studies consistently find it works about as well as sleeping pills in the short term and considerably better six and twelve months later, because it changes the loop rather than suppressing the symptom. It is typically four to eight sessions, delivered by a therapist, a clinic program, or a structured self-help course.

The components are unglamorous and specific:

  • Sleep restriction (better named sleep consolidation) — you temporarily shorten your time in bed to roughly the hours you actually sleep, which rebuilds sleep pressure and consolidates broken sleep, then widen it back as efficiency improves. Effective, but genuinely hard for the first week or two, and it should be done with a clinician if you drive professionally or have epilepsy or bipolar disorder.
  • Stimulus control — bed is for sleep and sex only. If you're awake and frustrated for roughly twenty minutes, get up, do something dull in dim light, and return when sleepy. This is what rebuilds the bed-equals-sleep association.
  • Cognitive work — dismantling the catastrophic arithmetic ("if I don't sleep now I'll ruin tomorrow") that turns a bad night into an anxious one.
  • Relaxation training — paced breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, body scans; useful, but a supporting act rather than the treatment.
  • Sleep hygiene — the light, caffeine, temperature and timing basics in our sleep hygiene guide. Necessary groundwork, and on its own rarely enough to resolve chronic insomnia.

Prescription sleep medication has a real place — short courses at a crisis point, or where CBT-I isn't available. But tolerance, next-day grogginess and rebound insomnia on stopping are common, which is why guidelines put it second. Melatonin is a body-clock signal rather than a sedative and helps most with jet lag and delayed sleep timing, not classic insomnia. Never start, stop, or change a dose on your own — that conversation belongs with your doctor or pharmacist.

When to talk to a doctor

Book an appointment if sleep problems have run three nights a week for three months or more, if daytime sleepiness is affecting your driving or your work, or if any of the following are in the picture:

  • Loud snoring, gasping, choking, or witnessed pauses in breathing — see sleep apnea signs.
  • An irresistible urge to move your legs in the evening, eased by moving them.
  • Persistent low mood, hopelessness, or anxiety alongside the sleep loss.
  • Acting out dreams, sleepwalking, or falling asleep abruptly during the day.
  • Insomnia that began or worsened right after a new medication.

Bring two weeks of records with you. A completed sleep diary — bedtimes, wake times, awakenings, how you felt the next day — is exactly what a clinician needs to tell the difference between insomnia, a body-clock disorder and a breathing problem, and it saves an appointment's worth of guesswork.

You do not need software to beat insomnia. A notebook by the bed and two minutes each morning covers everything CBT-I asks for, and plenty of people have recovered with exactly that. Frequently asked questions

What is the most common cause of insomnia?

Stress is the most common trigger, but in long-running insomnia the strongest driver is usually the set of habits that formed around it — extra time in bed, lie-ins, naps, and lying awake in bed frustrated. That is why treatment targets the habits rather than the original stressor.

How long does insomnia last?

Acute insomnia usually clears within days to a few weeks once its trigger passes. Insomnia is called chronic once it has run at least three nights a week for three months, and at that point it rarely resolves on its own — but it responds well to CBT-I, often within four to eight weeks.

Can't I just take something to sleep?

Short courses of prescribed medication have a legitimate role, especially at a crisis point, but guidelines put CBT-I first because its benefit holds up long after treatment ends. Tolerance, next-day grogginess and rebound insomnia on stopping are common with sedatives. Any decision about medication belongs with your doctor — never start or stop one on your own.

Does lying in bed awake help at all?

No — it actively works against you. Staying in bed awake teaches your brain to associate bed with wakefulness. If you're still awake and frustrated after roughly twenty minutes, get up, do something calm and dull in dim light, and go back when you feel sleepy. See how to fall asleep faster.

How do I know if it's insomnia or sleep apnea?

You often can't tell from the inside — both feel like broken, unrefreshing sleep. Snoring, gasping, witnessed breathing pauses, morning headaches and heavy daytime sleepiness point toward apnea; see sleep apnea signs. Only a doctor and, usually, a sleep study can settle it.
