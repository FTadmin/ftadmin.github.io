How to Get More Deep Sleep

Last updated: August 2026

Deep sleep — stage N3, or slow-wave sleep — is the heaviest part of the night: the stage where you're hardest to wake, where your brain waves slow to long rolling waves, and where most of the physical restoration happens. It's also the stage people most want more of, usually after a wearable told them their number looked low.

The good news is that a handful of things reliably increase it. The honest news is that the levers are fewer and smaller than the internet suggests, and the biggest one is simply sleeping enough in the first place. Here's what deep sleep does, what actually moves it, and what to expect.

What deep sleep actually does

A night runs through repeating cycles of roughly 90 minutes, each containing light sleep, deep sleep, and REM — the structure is laid out in our guide to sleep cycles and stages. Deep sleep is not spread evenly through those cycles: it is heavily front-loaded, with most of it in the first two or three cycles of the night. By the early morning hours you're getting mostly light sleep and REM.

During slow-wave sleep, several things happen that don't happen anywhere else in the same way:

  • Physical repair. The largest pulse of growth hormone in a 24-hour day is released during early-night deep sleep, supporting tissue repair, muscle recovery, and bone and immune function.
  • Memory consolidation. Slow-wave sleep is where the day's factual, explicit memories get stabilized and moved into longer-term storage. This is why a night of poor sleep before an exam undoes a day of studying.
  • Brain housekeeping. Clearance of metabolic waste from brain tissue is substantially more active during sleep than waking, and research links it particularly to slow-wave sleep — an active area of study rather than a settled story.
  • A nightly reset. Blood pressure and heart rate hit their 24-hour low during deep sleep, and short or fragmented sleep is consistently associated with worse blood-sugar control.

How much should you get? For most healthy adults, deep sleep is somewhere around 13–23% of total sleep time — very roughly one to two hours across a full night. That share falls naturally with age: deep sleep is abundant in children and adolescents, declines through adulthood, and can be minimal in older adults. That's normal biology, not a fault to be fixed.

The one thing that matters most: total sleep

Before any technique, the arithmetic. Because deep sleep is concentrated in the first half of the night, cutting your night short costs you mostly REM. But going to bed too late, sleeping too few hours night after night, or breaking sleep into fragments reduces the total slow-wave sleep you get and how consolidated it is.

Sleep pressure — the buildup of adenosine and the drive to sleep that grows over each waking hour — is what generates deep sleep. Two practical consequences:

  • Give yourself the opportunity. Most adults need 7–9 hours; see how much sleep you need. You can't concentrate deep sleep into five hours by trying harder.
  • Don't dilute the pressure. Long or late naps, and lying in bed for ten hours to get seven hours of sleep, both spread sleep drive thinner. A short early-afternoon nap is fine; a two-hour nap at 6 p.m. costs you tonight's deep sleep.

If you're chronically short on sleep, everything below is a rounding error by comparison. Fix the hours first.

What genuinely increases deep sleep

These four have the most consistent support, and conveniently they're all free.

Exercise — and its timing

Regular aerobic exercise is one of the few interventions that reliably increases slow-wave sleep, and studies consistently find the effect across a range of activity types. Moderate-to-vigorous aerobic work seems to do most of it; resistance training helps too. It's a habit effect more than a single-session one — the benefit builds over weeks of regular activity.

Timing matters less than the old advice suggested. A hard session in the hour before bed can delay sleep onset for some people, but moderate evening exercise beats skipping it. Morning or afternoon exercise has the bonus of anchoring your body clock, especially outdoors in daylight.

Temperature — cool room, warm start

Your body has to drop its core temperature by about a degree to enter and hold deep sleep, and a room that's too warm blocks that. A bedroom in roughly the 60–67°F / 16–19°C range suits most people; if you're waking sweaty, you're too warm.

The counterintuitive trick that works: a warm bath or shower one to two hours before bed. The warmth pulls blood to your skin, and the rapid heat loss afterwards accelerates the core-temperature drop that triggers sleep. Warm hands and feet, cool core, cool room.

A consistent schedule

Deep sleep is generated by two systems working together — sleep pressure and your circadian clock. When your bedtime and wake time wander by hours across the week, those two systems fall out of phase and sleep gets shallower and more fragmented even when the total hours look fine. The single highest-value habit is a fixed wake time, seven days a week, with morning daylight soon after. Bedtime then tends to sort itself out. More on the mechanics in our sleep hygiene guide.

Skipping the nightcap

Alcohol is the most effective deep-sleep suppressant most people use regularly. It's sedating, so it shortens the time it takes to fall asleep — which is exactly why it feels like it helps — but it fragments the second half of the night, suppresses REM, and reduces the quality and continuity of slow-wave sleep. Studies consistently find that even moderate evening drinking measurably worsens sleep architecture.

You don't have to be teetotal. Moving drinks earlier in the evening, keeping the amount modest, and leaving several alcohol-free nights a week produces a noticeable difference within a week or two. Late caffeine works on a different mechanism — it blocks the adenosine signal that builds sleep pressure — and has a long enough half-life that an afternoon coffee is still working at bedtime for many people.

Worth naming what doesn't work: there's no supplement with good evidence for increasing deep sleep in healthy adults. Melatonin is a timing signal, not a depth signal. Alcohol and most sedatives reduce it. And chronic sleep-disrupting conditions — untreated sleep apnea in particular — wreck slow-wave sleep in a way no habit change can compensate for.

Realistic expectations

Here's the part most articles skip. Deep sleep is largely self-regulating and strongly individual. Your brain prioritizes it: after a night of restriction, it takes the slow-wave sleep back first, before REM, in the following nights. That homeostatic push means the amount you get is closer to a body-set requirement than a score you can grind upward.

What that means in practice:

  • The ceiling is real. Good habits get you to your own healthy amount. They do not double it, and there's no evidence more slow-wave sleep than your body wants is better for you.
  • Age is not a bug. Deep sleep declines steadily across adulthood. A 60-year-old getting less N3 than they did at 25 is normal, not deteriorating.
  • Night-to-night variation is huge. One low night means very little. Look at two-week averages or don't look at all.
  • Consumer trackers estimate, they don't measure. Wearables infer stages from movement and heart-rate patterns; only a lab study reads brain waves directly. They're reasonable at total sleep time and much shakier at the stage split, so treat a wrist device's "deep sleep" figure as a trend line, not a verdict — anxiety about sleep scores is itself a well-documented way to sleep worse.

The honest measure of whether you're getting enough deep sleep isn't a percentage. It's whether you wake up feeling restored and stay alert through the day without fighting it. If you don't — and especially if you snore heavily, wake gasping, or feel exhausted after a full night — that's a conversation with a doctor, not a settings change. Persistent unrefreshing sleep is also one of the classic insomnia and sleep-apnea presentations.

Tracking what you can actually control — paper or app

You can't measure your own slow-wave sleep at home, and you don't need to. Track the inputs instead: bedtime, wake time, alcohol, caffeine, exercise, and how rested you felt. Two weeks of that tells you more than any estimated percentage.

A notebook does it — five lines each morning, and the pattern surfaces on its own. Most people find their worst mornings cluster around late nights, late drinks, or an irregular wake time, and seeing it written down is what changes behavior.

Frequently asked questions

How much deep sleep should I get a night?

For most healthy adults, roughly 13–23% of total sleep — very approximately one to two hours across a full night. The share declines naturally with age, so an older adult getting noticeably less is normal rather than a problem. Total sleep time matters far more than chasing a stage percentage.

Can I increase deep sleep with a supplement?

There's no supplement with good evidence for increasing slow-wave sleep in healthy adults. Melatonin shifts sleep *timing* rather than deepening sleep, and alcohol and most sedatives actively reduce deep sleep. Regular aerobic exercise, a cool bedroom, a consistent wake time and less evening alcohol are the levers that have real support.

Is my watch's deep sleep number accurate?

Treat it as an estimate. Wearables infer stages from movement and heart-rate patterns; only a lab sleep study measures brain waves directly. Trackers do reasonably well on total sleep time and much less well on the stage-by-stage split, so use the trend over weeks and ignore individual nights.

Does exercising at night ruin deep sleep?

Usually not. Regular aerobic exercise is one of the most reliable ways to increase slow-wave sleep, and for most people evening workouts are far better than skipping them. If a hard session in the hour before bed leaves you wired, move it earlier — but don't drop the exercise to protect your sleep.

Why do I wake up exhausted even after eight hours?

Common causes are fragmented sleep from an untreated breathing problem, alcohol late in the evening, an irregular schedule, or sleep inertia from waking mid-cycle. If it's persistent — especially with snoring, gasping, or morning headaches — see sleep apnea signs and talk to a doctor rather than trying to optimize it yourself.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Sleep-stage figures from consumer wearables are estimates and should never be used to diagnose anything — always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider. See a doctor if you regularly wake unrefreshed despite adequate sleep, if you snore loudly or wake gasping or choking, if you fall asleep uncontrollably during the day, or if daytime sleepiness is affecting your driving.
