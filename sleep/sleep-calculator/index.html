Sleep Calculator: When to Go to Bed

Last updated: August 2026

The idea behind a sleep calculator is simple. Your brain moves through sleep cycles of roughly 90 minutes, and waking at the end of a cycle — in light sleep — feels far better than being pulled out of the middle of one. So you fix your wake time, count backwards in 90-minute blocks, add about 15 minutes for falling asleep, and you have your bedtime.

Below are the tables for the most common alarm times, plus the part most calculators leave out: cycle length is an average, not a constant, and total sleep matters more than clever timing. Use the numbers as a starting point, then let a couple of weeks of your own records tell you which one actually works.

How the calculation works

Three ingredients, and only one of them is precise:

  1. Your wake time. This is the fixed point — the alarm you can't move.
  2. Cycle length. The standard estimate is 90 minutes. Real cycles run roughly 70–120 minutes and vary within a night, so this is an average standing in for a range.
  3. Sleep latency — how long you take to fall asleep after lights out. Healthy latency is 10–20 minutes; the tables below assume 15.

So: bedtime = wake time − (90 minutes × number of cycles) − 15 minutes. Five cycles gives 7.5 hours of sleep, six gives 9 hours. Both sit inside the recommended 7–9 hour adult range; four cycles is 6 hours and falls short for almost everyone, which is why it appears in the tables as a last resort rather than a plan.

If you routinely take much longer than 20 minutes to drop off, adjust the 15-minute allowance upward — or better, look at why it's taking so long, since a long latency is itself worth fixing.

Why the 90-minute number isn't exact

Every sleep calculator on the internet uses 90 minutes because it's the population average. Your own cycles may run closer to 75 or closer to 110, and they don't stay constant through the night — the first cycle is typically shortest, and later cycles get longer as REM periods stretch out. Layer on the normal brief awakenings between cycles, plus a sleep latency that changes with stress, caffeine and how tired you already were, and the arithmetic drifts by twenty or thirty minutes fairly easily.

What that means in practice:

  • Never trade total sleep for cycle alignment. Going to bed 45 minutes later so the math lands on a whole cycle is a bad deal — you lose real sleep to chase a rounding error.
  • Use it to break a tie. If 10:15 pm and 10:45 pm are both plausible, the cycle math is a reasonable reason to pick one.
  • Expect to adjust the number. If the 7½-hour option consistently leaves you groggy but 8 hours flat feels fine, your cycles aren't 90 minutes. Your experience beats the formula.
  • Consistency outranks precision. The same bedtime every night, including weekends, does more for how you feel at 7 am than any calculation.

Sleep inertia — the fog after a badly timed alarm — is real and can last half an hour, so the underlying idea is sound. It's the decimal-place confidence that isn't.

Making a new bedtime actually stick

Most people don't fail at knowing their bedtime; they fail at reaching it. A few things that reliably help:

  • Move in 15-minute steps. Shifting bedtime an hour earlier in one night usually means lying awake for an hour. Move by 15 minutes and hold it for three or four nights before the next step.
  • Anchor the wake time first. Your body clock is set far more strongly by when light hits your eyes in the morning than by when you turn the lamp off. Fix the wake time — every day, weekends included — and the bedtime tends to follow.
  • Set a reminder for the wind-down, not the bedtime. A prompt 30–45 minutes before lights out is the one that changes behavior; a prompt at bedtime itself just tells you you're already late.
  • Protect the last hour. Bright screens, hard exercise, heavy meals and alcohol all push sleep onset later — see sleep hygiene for the full checklist.
  • Don't lie in bed frustrated. If you're still awake after about 20 minutes, get up, do something dull in dim light, and go back when you feel sleepy.

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

A sleep calculator gives you a hypothesis; only your own record tests it. Two weeks of noting when you went to bed, when you woke and how you felt will tell you more about your ideal bedtime than any table — including this one. A notebook on the nightstand does that job fine, and if you'll use one, use it.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I go to bed to wake up at 6 am?

10:15 pm for five cycles (7½ hours of sleep) or 8:45 pm for six cycles (9 hours), both allowing 15 minutes to fall asleep. If you're regularly short on sleep, aim for the earlier one for a week and see how the mornings feel.

Are sleep calculators accurate?

Roughly. They all assume a 90-minute cycle, and real cycles run about 70–120 minutes and change through the night — so expect the estimate to drift by 20–30 minutes. Useful for choosing between two realistic bedtimes; not a reason to stay up later.

Is it better to sleep 6 hours or 7½ hours?

7½ hours, without question. Six hours is four cycles and falls below the recommended range for almost every adult — see how much sleep you need. Never sacrifice total sleep to make the cycle math come out even.

How long should it take to fall asleep?

10–20 minutes is typical and healthy. Falling asleep in under five minutes usually signals you're carrying a sleep debt. Regularly taking more than 30 minutes is worth addressing — start with how to fall asleep faster.

Should I use the same bedtime on weekends?

Yes, as close as you can manage — and protect the wake time especially. A two-hour weekend shift produces a jetlag-like effect that makes Sunday night hard and Monday worse. If you need extra sleep, a modest lie-in of under an hour costs you far less.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Sleep cycle length and sleep need vary between individuals, so treat any calculated bedtime as a starting point — always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider.
