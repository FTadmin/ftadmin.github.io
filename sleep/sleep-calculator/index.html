Sleep Calculator: When to Go to Bed

Last updated: August 2026

The idea is simple. Your brain moves through sleep cycles of roughly 90 minutes, and waking at the end of one — in light sleep — feels far better than being pulled out of the middle. Fix your wake time, count back in 90-minute blocks, add 15 minutes for falling asleep: that's your bedtime.

Below: tables for common alarm times, plus what most calculators leave out — cycle length is an average, and total sleep matters more than clever timing. Start with the numbers; let two weeks of your own records pick the winner.

How the calculation works

Three ingredients, only one of them precise:

  1. Your wake time — the alarm you can't move.
  2. Cycle length. The standard estimate is 90 minutes; real cycles run roughly 70–120 and vary within a night.
  3. Sleep latency — how long you take to fall asleep. Healthy is 10–20 minutes; the tables assume 15.

So: bedtime = wake time − (90 minutes × cycles) − 15 minutes. Five cycles is 7.5 hours, six is 9 — both inside the recommended 7–9 hour adult range. Four cycles is 6 hours, short for almost everyone — a last resort, not a plan.

Routinely need more than 20 minutes? Raise the allowance — or better, look at why it's taking so long — a long latency is itself worth fixing.

Why the 90-minute number isn't exact

Every calculator uses 90 minutes — the population average. Yours may run nearer 75 or 110, cycles lengthen through the night, and normal awakenings plus a latency that shifts with stress and caffeine drift the math by twenty or thirty minutes. In practice:

  • Never trade total sleep for cycle alignment. Staying up 45 minutes so the math lands on a whole cycle loses real sleep to a rounding error.
  • Use it to break a tie between two plausible bedtimes.
  • Expect to adjust. If 7½ hours consistently leaves you groggy but 8 flat feels fine, your cycles aren't 90 minutes. Your experience beats the formula.
  • Consistency outranks precision. The same bedtime every night, weekends included, does more for 7 am than any calculation.

Sleep inertia — the fog after a badly timed alarm — is real and can last half an hour, so the idea is sound. The decimal-place confidence isn't.

Making a new bedtime actually stick

Most people don't fail at knowing their bedtime; they fail at reaching it. What reliably helps:

  • Move in 15-minute steps. An hour earlier in one night means an hour lying awake. Hold each step three or four nights.
  • Anchor the wake time first. Morning light sets your body clock far more strongly than lamp-off time. Fix the wake time, every day, and the bedtime tends to follow.
  • Set the reminder for the wind-down, not the bedtime. A prompt 30–45 minutes before lights out changes behavior; one at bedtime just tells you you're late.
  • Protect the last hour. Bright screens, hard exercise, heavy meals and alcohol all push sleep onset later — see sleep hygiene.
  • Don't lie in bed frustrated. Still awake after about 20 minutes? Get up, do something dull in dim light, return when sleepy.

Frequently asked questions

What time should I go to bed to wake up at 6 am?

10:15 pm for five cycles (7½ hours of sleep) or 8:45 pm for six (9 hours), both allowing 15 minutes to fall asleep. If you're regularly short on sleep, try the earlier one for a week and see how the mornings feel.

Are sleep calculators accurate?

Roughly. They assume a 90-minute cycle; real cycles run about 70–120 minutes and change through the night, so expect the estimate to drift by 20–30 minutes. Useful for choosing between two realistic bedtimes; not a reason to stay up later.

Is it better to sleep 6 hours or 7½ hours?

7½ hours, without question. Six hours is four cycles and falls below the recommended range for almost every adult — see how much sleep you need. Never sacrifice total sleep to make the cycle math come out even.

How long should it take to fall asleep?

10–20 minutes is typical and healthy. Under five minutes usually signals sleep debt. Regularly over 30 minutes is worth addressing — start with how to fall asleep faster.

Should I use the same bedtime on weekends?

Yes, as close as you can — and protect the wake time especially. A two-hour weekend shift produces a jetlag-like effect that makes Sunday night hard and Monday worse. If you need extra sleep, a lie-in of under an hour costs far less.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Sleep cycle length and sleep need vary between individuals, so treat any calculated bedtime as a starting point — always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider. See a clinician if you consistently take more than 30 minutes to fall asleep, wake unrefreshed after a full night, fall asleep unintentionally during the day, or if a partner reports loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in your breathing.
