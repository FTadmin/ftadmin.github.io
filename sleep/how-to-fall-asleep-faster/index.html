How to Fall Asleep Faster

Last updated: August 2026

A healthy sleeper takes 10 to 20 minutes to fall asleep. Not two minutes — that usually means sleep debt — and not an hour, where the frustration starts. If you're regularly awake past the 30-minute mark, the fixes below have actual evidence behind them, in rough order of worth.

One thing first: you cannot make yourself fall asleep, and trying is precisely what keeps you awake. Everything useful here removes an obstacle — arousal, light, heat, or the bed–wakefulness association — rather than forcing the outcome. For the broader habit picture, see sleep hygiene.

The 20-minute rule: get out of bed

The most effective technique here, and the one people resist most. After about 20 minutes awake — estimate, don't clock-watch — get up. Another room, dim lights, something dull (a book, not your phone), back to bed only when genuinely sleepy. Repeat as needed.

It feels counterproductive and it works: every hour spent awake and frustrated in bed teaches your brain that bed means wakefulness. Breaking it — stimulus control — is a core component of CBT-I, the first-line treatment ahead of sleeping pills. Companion rules: bed only for sleep and sex; same wake time every morning regardless of the night. Sleeping in feels humane and quietly guarantees the next night is just as hard. If lying awake is your normal rather than your occasional, read insomnia: causes and what helps.

Temperature: the most underrated lever

Falling asleep depends on core temperature dropping: help the drop and sleep comes faster; block it and no tiredness compensates.

  • Keep the bedroom cool — roughly 16–19 °C (60–67 °F). Err cooler, add a blanket.
  • Warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed. Not a contradiction — warming the skin sends blood to the surface, and the rebound heat loss drops your core faster. One of the best-supported non-drug fixes.
  • Warm hands and feet. Cold extremities restrict the blood flow that dumps heat — socks in bed genuinely help some people. Small effect, free.
  • No hard exercise or heavy meals in the last 2–3 hours — both raise core temperature at the wrong moment. Earlier in the day, exercise helps.

Temperature is also why hot summer nights break up: your body fights to shed heat while REM sleep — where thermoregulation is impaired — dominates.

Light, and what actually happens with screens

Light is your body clock's strongest signal: bright evening light, blue-heavy screens especially, suppresses melatonin and pushes the clock later. But the popular version is overstated. At phone distance, brightness is a modest suppressor; the bigger cost is content — a doom-scroll, a work email, one more episode. Arousal beats melatonin as the reason you're awake at 1 am; night-shift filters fix the smaller half of the problem.

  • Dim the house for the last hour — lamps, not overheads. Does more than any filter.
  • Stop the stimulating content, not just the blue light.
  • Make the bedroom genuinely dark — blackout curtains or a mask; cover standby LEDs.
  • Get bright light in the morning — 10–20 minutes outdoors anchors the clock and, counterintuitively, helps you get sleepy on time tonight.

Breathing and relaxation: what the evidence supports

The honest picture: slow breathing reliably lowers arousal — heart rate drops, the nervous system shifts toward rest — which helps when arousal is the obstacle. No pattern is magic; nothing puts you to sleep in a fixed number of seconds.

  • 4-7-8 breathing: inhale 4 counts, hold 7, exhale 8; four cycles. The long exhale is the active ingredient; if the hold makes you tense, shorten everything and keep the exhale longest.
  • Progressive muscle relaxation: tense each muscle group five seconds, release, move feet to face. Among the better-studied methods; it gives a racing mind something dull to hold.
  • Cognitive shuffling: pick a random word, picture an unrelated object for each letter. Occupies the verbal, planning mind so it can't rehearse tomorrow. Little research, plenty of anecdote, no downside.

Skip anything that turns falling asleep into a performance — "clearing your mind" (a task, therefore arousing) and counting the minutes awake included.

Frequently asked questions

How long should it take to fall asleep?

10–20 minutes is normal. Under five minutes usually means sleep deprivation, not talent. Regularly over 30 minutes is worth doing something about — start with the 20-minute rule and a consistent wake time.

Does the military sleep method work in two minutes?

Treat the two-minute claim as folklore — it comes from an old training manual, not a study. The components (progressive muscle relaxation plus a calm mental image) are legitimate; the stopwatch promise isn't, and holding yourself to it just adds pressure.

What should I do if I wake at 3 am and can't get back to sleep?

The same 20-minute rule. Don't lie there fighting it: get up, dim light, something dull, back to bed when sleepy. Keep your normal wake time the next morning even after a short night — that protects the following one. See insomnia: causes and what helps if it's a recurring pattern.

Do sleeping pills help you fall asleep faster?

They shorten sleep onset, usually by less than people expect, and the effect fades with regular use while rebound insomnia on stopping does not. Clinical guidelines put CBT-I ahead of medication for chronic insomnia. Any decision about sleep medication belongs with your doctor.

Does reading in bed help or hurt?

It depends on the bed association. A few pages of something undemanding that ends in sleep is fine. Reading for an hour because you're not sleepy uses the bed for wakefulness — better to read in a chair and move to bed when your eyes start closing.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Never start, stop, or change any medication — including over-the-counter sleep aids — without speaking to your doctor or healthcare provider. See a clinician if difficulty falling asleep persists for more than a few weeks, if it affects your daytime functioning or mood, if you fall asleep unintentionally during the day, or if a partner reports loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in your breathing.
