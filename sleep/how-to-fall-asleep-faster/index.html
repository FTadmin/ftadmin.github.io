How to Fall Asleep Faster

Last updated: August 2026

A healthy sleeper takes 10 to 20 minutes to fall asleep. Not two minutes — that usually means you're carrying a sleep debt — and not an hour, which is where the frustration starts. If you're regularly lying awake past the 30-minute mark, the fixes below are the ones with actual evidence behind them, roughly in order of how much they're worth.

One thing to get straight first: you cannot make yourself fall asleep. Sleep is not a task you can try harder at, and trying is precisely what keeps you awake. Everything useful here works by removing an obstacle — arousal, light, heat, or the association between your bed and lying awake — rather than by forcing the outcome. If you want the broader habit picture, start with sleep hygiene.

The 20-minute rule: get out of bed

This is the single most effective technique on the page, and the one people resist most. If you've been lying awake for about 20 minutes — don't clock-watch, just estimate — get up. Go to another room, keep the lights dim, do something quiet and undemanding (a dull book, folding laundry, not your phone), and return to bed only when you feel genuinely sleepy. Repeat as many times as the night requires.

It feels counterproductive and it works, because it targets the mechanism that turns one bad night into a habit. Every hour you spend awake and frustrated in bed trains your brain to associate the bed with wakefulness and anxiety. Break that association and sleep onset improves — this technique, called stimulus control, is a core component of cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which is the first-line treatment clinicians recommend ahead of sleeping pills.

The companion rules matter too: use the bed only for sleep and sex, and get up at the same time every morning regardless of how the night went. Sleeping in to compensate feels humane and quietly guarantees the next night is just as hard. If lying awake is your normal rather than your occasional, read insomnia: causes and what helps.

Temperature: the most underrated lever

Falling asleep depends on your core body temperature dropping. Anything that helps that drop happen speeds sleep onset; anything that blocks it delays sleep no matter how tired you are.

  • Keep the bedroom cool — roughly 16–19 °C (60–67 °F) suits most people. If you're unsure, err cooler and add a blanket.
  • Take a warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed. This is not a contradiction: warming your skin sends blood to the surface, and the rebound loss of heat afterward drops your core temperature faster than it would otherwise. It's one of the better-supported non-drug interventions for sleep onset.
  • Warm your hands and feet. Cold extremities restrict the blood flow that dumps heat. Socks in bed genuinely help some people fall asleep faster — a small effect, but free.
  • Avoid hard exercise and heavy meals in the last 2–3 hours, both of which raise core temperature at exactly the wrong moment. Earlier in the day, exercise does the opposite and helps.

Temperature is also why the second half of a hot summer night is so broken: your body is fighting to shed heat during the stretch when REM sleep normally dominates, and thermoregulation is impaired in REM.

Light, and what actually happens with screens

Light is the strongest signal your body clock has. Bright evening light — especially the blue-heavy light of overhead LEDs and screens — suppresses melatonin release and pushes your internal clock later, so you're not sleepy when you want to be.

But be precise about the mechanism, because the popular version is overstated. Screen brightness at typical phone distance is a modest melatonin suppressor; the bigger cost is usually content. A doom-scroll, a work email, or one more episode keeps you cognitively and emotionally aroused, and arousal beats melatonin as a reason you're still awake at 1 am. Night-shift filters address the smaller half of the problem.

What to do:

  • Dim the house for the last hour — lamps instead of overheads. This does more than any screen filter.
  • Stop the stimulating content, not just the blue light. If you must use a screen, make it something dull.
  • Make the bedroom genuinely dark. Blackout curtains or an eye mask; cover standby LEDs.
  • Get bright light in the morning — 10–20 minutes outdoors soon after waking anchors the clock and, counterintuitively, is one of the more reliable ways to make yourself sleepy at the right time tonight.

Breathing and relaxation: what the evidence supports

Slow breathing techniques are widely recommended and the picture is honest but modest: controlled slow breathing reliably reduces physiological arousal — heart rate drops, the nervous system shifts toward its rest state — and that helps if arousal is what's keeping you awake. What the evidence does not support is any specific pattern being magic, or the claim that a method puts you to sleep in a fixed number of seconds.

4-7-8 breathing

Inhale through the nose for 4 counts, hold for 7, exhale slowly through the mouth for 8. Repeat four cycles. The long exhale is the active ingredient; the exact counts are not sacred, and if holding for 7 makes you tense, shorten everything and keep the exhale longer than the inhale.

Progressive muscle relaxation

Tense a muscle group for about five seconds, release, notice the difference, then move on — feet upward to face. Slow, systematic, and among the better-studied relaxation methods for sleep. It also gives a racing mind something dull to hold onto.

Cognitive shuffling

Pick a random word, then picture an unrelated object for each letter in turn. The point is to occupy the verbal, planning part of your mind with something meaningless, so it can't rehearse tomorrow's meeting. Little formal research, plenty of anecdote, and no downside.

What to skip: forcing yourself to "clear your mind" (a task, therefore arousing), counting how long you've been awake, and anything that turns falling asleep into a performance you can fail at.

Notebook vs app: finding your own obstacle

All of the above is a menu, and your particular obstacle is on it somewhere — but which one is an empirical question, not something an article can tell you. The answer comes from a few weeks of records: bedtime, roughly when you got to sleep, wake time, how the night rated, and a note about what the evening involved. Two weeks in and the pattern usually announces itself — the 4 pm coffee, the late gym session, the nights you took work to bed.

Frequently asked questions

How long should it take to fall asleep?

10–20 minutes is normal. Under five minutes usually means you're sleep-deprived rather than a good sleeper. Regularly over 30 minutes is worth doing something about — start with the 20-minute rule and a consistent wake time.

Does the military sleep method work in two minutes?

Treat the two-minute claim as folklore — it comes from an old training manual, not a study. The components (progressive muscle relaxation plus a calm mental image) are legitimate and worth using; the stopwatch promise isn't, and holding yourself to it just adds pressure.

What should I do if I wake at 3 am and can't get back to sleep?

The same 20-minute rule applies. Don't lie there fighting it: get up, dim light, something dull, back to bed when sleepy. Keep your normal wake time the next morning even if the night was short — that's what protects the following night. See insomnia: causes and what helps if it's a recurring pattern.

Do sleeping pills help you fall asleep faster?

They shorten sleep onset, but usually by less than people expect, and the effect fades with regular use while the rebound insomnia on stopping does not. Clinical guidelines put CBT-I ahead of medication as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia. Any decision about sleep medication belongs with your doctor.

Does reading in bed help or hurt?

It depends on what it does to the bed association. If you read a few pages of something undemanding and fall asleep, it's fine. If you read for an hour because you're not sleepy, the bed is being used for wakefulness — better to read in a chair and move to bed when your eyes start closing.

Medical disclaimer
