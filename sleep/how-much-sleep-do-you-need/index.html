How Much Sleep Do You Need?

Last updated: August 2026

Most healthy adults need 7 to 9 hours of sleep a night. That's the short answer, and for the majority of people it's the whole answer. But the number shifts a lot across a lifetime — a newborn needs more than twice what their grandparent does — and it shifts a little between two adults of the same age.

Below is the full chart by age group, the signs that tell you you're running short regardless of what the chart says, and a simple way to find your own number using a couple of weeks of sleep diary entries rather than guesswork.

Recommended hours of sleep by age

These ranges reflect the consensus recommendations used by sleep medicine bodies and pediatric guidelines. For children and teenagers the figures include naps; for adults they refer to a single overnight block.

Age groupRecommended sleepNotes
Newborn (0–3 months)14–17 hoursBroken into short stretches around the clock
Infant (4–11 months)12–15 hoursNight sleep consolidates; 1–3 naps
Toddler (1–2 years)11–14 hoursUsually one afternoon nap
Preschool (3–5 years)10–13 hoursNaps often stop toward the end of this range
School age (6–12 years)9–12 hoursEarly school starts are the usual squeeze
Teen (13–18 years)8–10 hoursBody clock shifts later; most teens fall short
Adult (18–64 years)7–9 hoursThe standard adult range
Older adult (65+)7–8 hoursNeed barely drops; sleep gets lighter and more broken

Two things people commonly get wrong here. First, the older-adult row is not permission to sleep five hours — the requirement changes very little after 65, what changes is the ability to get it in one unbroken piece. Second, these are ranges for time asleep, not time in bed. Most people spend 20–40 minutes in bed awake across a night, so a 7.5-hour sleep usually means closer to 8 hours between lights out and the alarm.

Signs you're not getting enough

The chart is a starting point; your body is the real evidence. Chronic short sleep is easy to miss because the tiredness stops feeling like tiredness and starts feeling like your personality. Look for these:

  • You need an alarm every single day — and hit snooze more than once. Someone sleeping enough often wakes shortly before it.
  • You sleep dramatically longer on days off. A two-hour weekend lie-in is your body reclaiming a debt built up Monday to Friday.
  • You fall asleep within five minutes of your head hitting the pillow. That's not being "a great sleeper" — falling asleep normally takes 10–20 minutes.
  • Afternoon crashes, especially the sort you push through with caffeine after 3pm.
  • Micro-sleeps — losing a second or two in a meeting, or arriving somewhere in the car with no memory of the last mile. This one is dangerous and worth acting on immediately.
  • Irritability, low mood, and a short fuse out of proportion to what's happening.
  • Foggy recall and slow decisions — re-reading the same paragraph, losing words mid-sentence.
  • More frequent colds, or a heavier appetite and stronger cravings for fast carbohydrates.

Studies consistently find that people underestimate how impaired they are when short on sleep: performance keeps declining while the feeling of sleepiness plateaus. If several of the signs above are familiar, trust the list over your own sense of coping.

Why your number might not be 8

"Eight hours" is a convenient average, not a rule. Within the 7–9 range, where you sit is largely genetic and fairly stable across adulthood. A small number of people are genuine short sleepers who function well on around 6 hours; a similar number are long sleepers who really do need 9 or more. Both are far rarer than the number of people who claim to be short sleepers.

Several things legitimately push your need to the top of the range for a while:

  • Hard physical training — recovery load raises sleep need, often noticeably in the days after a long run or a heavy week.
  • Illness and recovery — sleeping more while fighting an infection is your immune system working, not laziness.
  • Pregnancy, particularly the first trimester.
  • Adolescence — teenagers need 8–10 hours and have a body clock that runs late, a combination school timetables handle badly.
  • Any accumulated debt from previous short nights.

To find your own figure, take a stretch where you can wake without an alarm — a holiday works well — and record when you fall asleep and wake for two weeks. The first few nights will run long as you pay off debt; the number your body settles on after that is your baseline. Sleep debt: can you catch up?

Partly. Sleep debt is real and it accumulates: six hours a night from Monday to Friday leaves you roughly ten hours short by Saturday. A long weekend lie-in genuinely recovers some of that — alertness and mood improve, and the body prioritizes the deepest, most restorative stages first when catching up. That's why recovery sleep often feels disproportionately good; see sleep cycles and stages for what's actually happening.

What catch-up doesn't do is make the debt free. Research consistently finds that measures like attention and metabolic markers don't fully return to baseline after a weekend of recovery sleep, and the pattern of short weekdays plus long weekends creates a jetlag-like swing in your body clock that makes Sunday night harder and Monday morning worse. The costs of chronic short sleep, in other words, aren't fully refundable.

A better approach than repaying: shrink the debt at the source. Move bedtime 15–20 minutes earlier at a time rather than attempting an hour in one jump, keep your wake time steady across all seven days, and let the weekend lie-in shrink on its own as the weekday deficit disappears. If you're getting to bed on time and still can't fall asleep, that's a different problem — see how to fall asleep faster.

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

Finding your personal sleep need takes one thing: two weeks of honest records. A notebook by the bed does that perfectly well, and if you'll actually write in it, use it. But the notebook has a predictable failure mode — you skip the night you got in late, which is exactly the night that mattered, and after a fortnight you're left doing arithmetic on scribbled times to work out your average.

An app handles the boring parts:

  • Sleep and wake times with elapsed time slept computed for you, so "how much did I actually average?" is a number, not a homework assignment.

  • A sleep-quality rating plus dream and note fields, so you can see whether more hours are actually buying you better nights.

Frequently asked questions

Is 6 hours of sleep enough?

For almost everyone, no. True short sleepers who function well on 6 hours are rare, and studies consistently find that people restricted to 6 hours keep declining on attention and reaction tests while *feeling* like they have adapted. If you rely on an alarm, crash in the afternoon, or sleep much longer on days off, 6 hours isn't enough for you.

Do older adults need less sleep?

Barely. The recommendation drops only from 7–9 hours to about 7–8 hours after 65. What changes is sleep *architecture* — nights get lighter and more fragmented, and the body clock shifts earlier — so many older adults spend longer in bed to get the same amount of sleep. Persistent daytime sleepiness is worth raising with a doctor rather than accepting as part of aging.

How much sleep do teenagers need?

8–10 hours, and most don't get it. Adolescence shifts the body clock later, so a teenager genuinely can't fall asleep at 9pm, and early school start times cut the other end. Protecting the wake-up time on weekends and keeping screens out of the last hour help more than lecturing about bedtime — see sleep hygiene.

Can I train myself to need less sleep?

No. You can train yourself to *tolerate* less sleep — the subjective sleepiness fades — but the measurable impairment doesn't. Sleep need is largely genetic and stable through adulthood. What you can do is make the hours you get more efficient by fixing the things that fragment them.

How do I know if I'm sleeping enough?

The cleanest test: on a stretch with no alarm, how long do you sleep once the first few catch-up nights are behind you? That's your number. Log two weeks in a sleep diary and compare it to what you're actually getting on a work night — the gap is your weekday debt.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Sleep needs vary between individuals, and persistent sleep problems deserve professional attention — always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider. See a clinician if you regularly sleep a full night and still wake unrefreshed, if you fall asleep unintentionally during the day, or if a partner reports loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in your breathing, which can be signs of a treatable sleep disorder.
