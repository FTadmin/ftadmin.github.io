Sleep Apnea Signs and Symptoms

Last updated: August 2026

Obstructive sleep apnea is a breathing problem that only happens while you're asleep: the muscles holding your airway open relax, the airway narrows or closes, and breathing stops for seconds at a time until your brain nudges you awake enough to reopen it. That can happen dozens of times an hour, all night, without you remembering a single one.

Which is why the condition is so easy to miss. The symptoms split neatly into two halves — the ones a bed partner sees, and the ones you feel the next day — and this guide covers both, plus who is most at risk and what actually happens when you ask a doctor about it. Nothing here diagnoses anything: sleep apnea is confirmed by a sleep study, not by a checklist.

Night-time signs and daytime signs

The night-time column is usually reported by somebody else. The daytime column is what finally sends people to a doctor — often years later.

Night-time signs (often noticed by a partner)Daytime signs (what you feel)
Loud, chronic snoring — often described as sawing, and loud enough to be heard through a wallExcessive daytime sleepiness: dozing off reading, watching TV, in meetings, or at traffic lights
Witnessed pauses in breathing, followed by a gasp, snort, or choking soundWaking unrefreshed no matter how many hours you spent in bed
Restless, thrashing sleep; sheets pulled apart by morningMorning headache, usually dull and frontal, easing over the first hour or two
Sudden awakenings with a feeling of breathlessnessDry mouth or a sore throat on waking
Getting up two or more times a night to urinate (nocturia)Trouble concentrating, forgetfulness, or slowed thinking
Sweating heavily at nightIrritability, low mood, or a shorter fuse than usual
Breathing pauses that end in a body jerkReduced interest in sex

Two of those deserve a note. Snoring alone is not sleep apnea — plenty of people snore loudly and breathe perfectly well all night, and some people with apnea barely snore at all. It's the combination — snoring plus witnessed pauses plus daytime sleepiness — that raises the suspicion. And nocturia is one of the most under-recognized signs: repeated nightly bathroom trips are commonly blamed on the bladder or on drinking too late, when interrupted breathing can be the underlying driver.

Apnea also frequently masquerades as insomnia. You wake repeatedly, can't say why, and conclude you're a bad sleeper — which is why it's worth ruling out before working through the usual insomnia causes.

Who is most at risk

Sleep apnea can affect anyone, including people who are slim and fit. But some factors raise the odds considerably:

  • Excess weight — the single strongest modifiable risk factor; fatty tissue around the neck and throat narrows the airway. Notably, weight loss meaningfully improves apnea for many people.
  • Neck circumference — a thicker neck means a narrower airway, independent of overall size.
  • Being male — men are diagnosed more often, though the gap narrows sharply after menopause and women's symptoms are more often missed because they present as fatigue, insomnia, or low mood rather than classic snoring.
  • Age — risk climbs through middle age.
  • Anatomy — a receding or small jaw, large tonsils or adenoids (the usual cause in children), a deviated septum, chronic nasal congestion.
  • Family history — partly anatomical, and it runs in families.
  • Alcohol, sedatives, and smoking — alcohol and sedatives relax the airway muscles, making collapse more likely; smoking inflames and narrows the airway.
  • Sleeping on your back — gravity pulls the tongue and soft palate backward. For some people apnea is largely positional.
  • Related conditions — high blood pressure (particularly when it resists treatment), type 2 diabetes, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and hypothyroidism all travel with sleep apnea in both directions.

There is also central sleep apnea, which is far less common: here the airway is open, but the brain briefly stops sending the signal to breathe. It shows up more often alongside heart failure, stroke, or opioid use, and it's diagnosed by the same sleep study.

Why it's worth taking seriously

The obvious cost is exhaustion — and it's not a small one. Untreated sleep apnea is consistently associated with a higher risk of road accidents, and sleepiness at the wheel is a legitimate reason to raise it with a doctor sooner rather than later.

The less visible cost is cardiovascular. Every apnea event drops blood oxygen and triggers a stress response that spikes blood pressure and heart rate, and repeating that hundreds of times a night, for years, is strongly associated with hypertension, arrhythmias, heart disease, stroke, and worse blood-sugar control. Sleep apnea is one of the recognized causes of blood pressure that won't come down despite treatment.

The encouraging half: it's one of the most treatable sleep disorders there is. Effective therapy — most commonly CPAP, sometimes a dental appliance, positional therapy, weight loss, or surgery for specific anatomical problems — often produces a change in daytime alertness within days. People routinely describe it as getting a decade back.

Getting a real answer: see a doctor, get a sleep study

You cannot diagnose sleep apnea yourself, and neither can a phone app, a smart ring, or a snoring recorder. Those tools can be a useful prompt — they are not a test. Diagnosis requires measuring breathing, oxygen levels, and effort while you sleep, and grading the result.

The path usually looks like this:

  1. See your doctor and describe both columns of symptoms. Bring your partner's observations — witnessed pauses carry real weight — or bring them along to the appointment.
  2. Screening questions. Most clinicians use a short structured questionnaire (snoring, tiredness, observed apneas, blood pressure, plus body measurements and age) to decide how urgently to test.
  3. A sleep study. Either a home sleep apnea test — a small kit with a nasal cannula, a finger oximeter, and a chest belt, worn in your own bed — or an in-lab polysomnography night, which measures far more and is used where the picture is complicated or central apnea is suspected.
  4. A severity grade based on events per hour of sleep, roughly: mild, moderate, or severe. Treatment recommendations follow from that plus your symptoms.

Bring records. A completed sleep diary covering two weeks — the hours you were actually in bed, how often you woke, how sleepy you were each day — helps your doctor separate apnea from short sleep, shift work, and insomnia, and gives a baseline to compare against once treatment starts.

Don't wait if you are falling asleep while driving, someone has watched you stop breathing, you wake gasping or choking, or you have chest pain or an irregular heartbeat at night. Those are reasons to be seen promptly.

What a sleep diary adds — paper or app

A sleep study measures one or two nights in enormous detail. A diary measures many nights in a little detail, and the two answer different questions: the study says whether your airway collapses, the diary says what your sleep pattern and daytime function actually look like week to week. Clinics ask for both.

A pocket notebook does this perfectly well — time to bed, time awake, wake-ups, a sleepiness score out of ten, anything notable. If that's what you'll keep, keep that. Where it falls down is arithmetic and continuity: calculating fourteen nights of elapsed sleep by hand, spotting that your worst days follow your latest nights, or finding the notebook three months later when the clinic asks how treatment is going.

Frequently asked questions

Can you have sleep apnea without snoring?

Yes. Snoring is the best-known sign, not a required one — some people with apnea are quiet sleepers, and it's more common for women's apnea to present as fatigue, insomnia, or low mood than as loud snoring. Judge on the whole picture: unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness, morning headaches, and nighttime bathroom trips.

Can an app or smartwatch diagnose sleep apnea?

No. Consumer devices and phone recordings can flag snoring or a dip in an estimated oxygen reading, and that can be a useful nudge to see a doctor — but a diagnosis requires a sleep study that measures airflow, effort and oxygen properly, interpreted by a clinician. Treat any app result as a prompt, never an answer.

Does sleep apnea only affect people who are overweight?

No. Excess weight is the strongest modifiable risk factor, but slim, fit people get sleep apnea too — often for anatomical reasons like a small or receding jaw, large tonsils, or chronic nasal obstruction. Being at a healthy weight doesn't rule it out.

How is sleep apnea treated?

Most often with CPAP, which holds the airway open with gently pressurized air; see CPAP therapy for what the first weeks are really like. Other options include a custom dental appliance for mild to moderate cases, positional therapy, weight loss, treating nasal obstruction, and surgery for specific anatomical problems. Your clinician chooses based on your study results.

Will a sleep study mean spending a night in a hospital?

Often not. Many people are tested with a home sleep apnea kit worn in their own bed. An in-lab study is used when the picture is complicated, when central apnea or another sleep disorder is suspected, or when a home test comes back inconclusive.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Sleep apnea can only be diagnosed by a doctor, usually with a sleep study — no symptom list, app, or wearable can confirm or rule it out. Always follow the guidance of your doctor or healthcare provider. Seek medical help promptly if you fall asleep while driving, if someone has seen you stop breathing in your sleep, or if you wake with chest pain, a pounding irregular heartbeat, or severe breathlessness.
