CPAP Therapy: Adjusting and Tracking

Last updated: August 2026

CPAP — continuous positive airway pressure — is the standard treatment for obstructive sleep apnea, and it works by doing something wonderfully unsubtle: a quiet blower sends gently pressurized air through a hose and mask, and that pressure acts as a splint holding your airway open so it can't collapse. No medication, no surgery, and the effect is immediate on the nights you use it.

The catch is entirely human. The therapy is highly effective and the equipment is easy to abandon, and most people who give up do so in the first few weeks over problems that have straightforward fixes. This guide covers those fixes, and why your clinic — and often your insurer — will be looking at your nightly usage numbers.

What the first weeks are actually like

Expect an adjustment period. A minority of people sleep well the first night; far more take two to six weeks before the mask stops being a foreign object. That is normal, and it is not a sign the therapy is wrong for you.

Three things make that stretch much shorter:

  • Wear it while you're awake. An hour on the sofa with the machine running, watching TV, breaks the association between mask and bedtime anxiety faster than anything else. Do it for a few evenings before you rely on it for a whole night.
  • Use it every night, all night, from the start. Partial use is the road to giving up: half-nights leave you still tired, which makes the machine feel pointless. Aim for the whole night even if the first few are rough.
  • Call your provider early. Mask fit, pressure settings, ramp, and humidification are all adjustable, and equipment suppliers expect to swap masks in the first month. Struggling quietly for six weeks is the single most common mistake.

The payoff is often quick. Many people notice sharper mornings and less daytime sleepiness within days to a couple of weeks — and if you don't, that's information worth bringing back to your clinician rather than a reason to stop.

Common problems and what fixes them

Nearly every early CPAP problem falls into one of these buckets, and nearly all of them are solvable. Work through the self-help column first; anything involving pressure settings belongs to your clinician.

Mask leak — air hissing, blowing into your eyes, waking to a loose maskWrong size or style, over-tightened straps, worn cushion, or the mask shifting when you turn overGet re-fitted — size is not the same across styles. Fit the mask lying down, in your sleeping position. Tighten less, not more: overtightening deforms the cushion and causes leaks. Replace cushions on schedule; silicone flattens out. A mask liner or a different style (nasal pillows vs nasal vs full-face) often solves it outright.

Two things you should not do on your own: change your prescribed pressure settings, and stop therapy because you feel better. Sleep apnea returns the night you stop.

Cleaning and replacement, briefly

Maintenance is unglamorous and takes about two minutes a day. Wipe the mask cushion daily with mild soap and water — facial oils are the main cause of the slow leak that appears after a few weeks. Wash the cushion, headgear, and hose weekly, empty and refill the humidifier chamber with distilled water daily, and check the filter monthly.

Parts wear out on a schedule, and your supplier will tell you theirs: cushions most often, headgear and hose less often, filters regularly. Skip ozone and UV "cleaner" gadgets unless your equipment provider specifically endorses one — soap and water is what manufacturers actually recommend, and some of those devices can damage the materials or void a warranty.

Why nightly usage gets tracked

Every modern CPAP machine records how long it ran, and that number matters to more people than you might expect.

For your clinic, usage is the first thing they look at when therapy isn't working. If you feel no better, the question is always whether you're getting enough hours to expect a benefit — and whether the nights you skip line up with something (a cold, travel, a new mask, alcohol, a bad-fit week). Usage plus your own notes turns a vague "it's not helping" into a specific, fixable problem.

For insurers and health systems, continued coverage of the machine and supplies frequently depends on documented adherence during an initial trial period — commonly something like four or more hours a night on the majority of nights over a set window. The exact rule varies by country, insurer, and provider, so check yours, but the pattern is near-universal: you're expected to demonstrate you're using it.

For you, the useful part isn't compliance at all — it's the pairing. Machine hours tell you how long it ran; a diary tells you how you slept and how you felt the following day. Together they answer the only question that really matters: is this working? A fortnight of hours next to a fortnight of morning sleepiness scores shows the trend far better than memory ever does, and gives you a genuine baseline from before therapy started if you kept a sleep diary beforehand.

Keep the basics going too. CPAP treats the airway; it doesn't override a 2 a.m. bedtime or three coffees at 6 p.m. The habits in our sleep hygiene guide still do their share of the work.

Logging it: machine data, paper, or app

Your machine and its app already store nightly hours, and if that's all you need, you're covered. What they don't hold is the human half: how rested you felt, whether the mask leaked, the night you gave up at 3 a.m. and why, the week your congestion made it unbearable. Manufacturer apps also lock the record inside one vendor's ecosystem, which is awkward the day you switch machines.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get used to CPAP?

Most people need somewhere between two and six weeks. Wearing the mask for an hour while awake before you sleep in it, using it every night rather than half-nights, and asking for a re-fit early all shorten that considerably. If you're still fighting it after a month, that's a call to your provider, not a reason to stop.

How many hours a night do I need to use it?

All night, every night, is the goal — the apnea comes back the moment the pressure stops. Insurers and health systems commonly set a documented minimum during an initial trial period (often around four hours on most nights), but that's a coverage floor, not a health target. The exact rule depends on your country, insurer and provider.

Why does CPAP make me bloated and burp?

That's aerophagia — air going down the esophagus instead of the airway. It often means the pressure is higher than you currently need, or that exhaling against it makes you swallow. Ask your clinician about a pressure review or exhale-relief settings; sleeping on your side or slightly propped up helps too. Don't adjust prescribed settings yourself.

Can I stop CPAP once I feel better?

No — feeling better is the therapy working, not the apnea resolving. Stopping brings the breathing pauses back the same night. If weight loss, surgery, or a change in circumstances has you wondering whether you still need it, that's a re-test question for your doctor, not a decision to make at home.

Is there an alternative to CPAP?

Sometimes. Custom dental (mandibular advancement) appliances work for many people with mild to moderate apnea, positional therapy helps when apnea is mostly back-sleeping, weight loss can substantially reduce severity, and surgery addresses specific anatomical problems. Which applies depends on your sleep study results and your clinician's assessment.

Medical disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. CPAP is a prescribed therapy: never change your pressure settings, switch equipment, or stop using your machine without talking to the clinician who prescribed it. Always follow the guidance of your doctor, sleep clinic, or equipment provider. Seek medical help promptly if you develop chest pain, severe breathlessness, persistent nosebleeds, or an eye infection you think may be related to mask leak.
