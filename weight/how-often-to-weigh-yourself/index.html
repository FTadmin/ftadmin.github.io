How Often Should You Weigh Yourself?

Last updated: August 2026

The honest answer is that there is no single correct frequency - there's a trade-off, and which side of it you want depends on what you're doing and how you respond to numbers. Weighing daily gives you a far better picture of your trend. Weighing weekly gives you a much calmer relationship with the scale. Both are defensible, and one of them is clearly wrong for some people.

This guide lays out the evidence on each side, gives you a straight comparison of daily, weekly and monthly weighing, and is specific about the situations where weighing more often is actively unhelpful. Whichever you pick, the technique matters more than the frequency - and so does knowing that day-to-day fluctuation of 1-2 kg (2-4 lb) is normal.

The short answer

If you're actively trying to change your weight and the scale doesn't upset you: weigh daily, but only ever judge the weekly average. You get seven data points instead of one, which makes the trend line dramatically more reliable, and you get a daily feedback loop that keeps the habit visible.

If the number affects your mood, or you're maintaining rather than changing: weigh once a week, same day, same conditions. You lose some resolution and gain a lot of peace, and for maintenance that's a good trade.

If weighing has ever been part of a disordered relationship with food or your body: weigh rarely or not at all, and let a professional help you decide. The rest of this guide explains why each of those answers holds.

The case for weighing daily

Research on self-monitoring is unusually consistent on one point: people who track their weight regularly tend to do better at losing weight and, more importantly, at keeping it off, than people who don't. Frequent self-weighing is one of the habits that repeatedly shows up among people who maintain a loss long-term. The mechanism isn't mysterious.

  • Better data, by a wide margin. A weekly weigh-in is a single reading, and a single reading carries all the water noise of that particular morning. Seven readings averaged cancel most of that noise out. If you weigh weekly and happen to land on a salty Monday, your whole week's "progress" is fiction.
  • Faster feedback on what you're doing. A trend that flattens for three weeks is visible much earlier with daily data, which is the difference between adjusting in month one and discovering it in month three.
  • Catching regain early. After a loss, the drift back up is slow and easy to miss in clothes. People who weigh regularly notice a 2 kg drift and act on it; people who don't tend to notice at 7 kg.
  • Desensitization. Counterintuitively, seeing the number bounce around every day teaches you that it bounces around. Weekly weighers often read every wobble as meaningful, because they see so few of them.

The critical condition on all of this: daily weighing only works if you commit to never interpreting a single day. Daily data, weekly judgement. That's what the weight loss chart and log template is built around.

The case for weighing weekly - and a comparison

Weekly weighing has one enormous advantage: it doesn't ask you to ignore anything. There's no daily temptation to over-read a number, no morning where the scale sets the tone. Studies on frequent weighing generally find neutral-to-positive effects on mood on average, but averages hide the people at the tails, and for some people a daily number is genuinely corrosive.

Weekly weighing also becomes more defensible the closer you are to maintenance. When you're not trying to change anything, you don't need the resolution - you just need to know whether you've drifted.

When weighing often is harmful rather than helpful

This is the part most articles skip. Frequent weighing is a tool, and it's the wrong tool in several situations:

  • If you have, or have had, an eating disorder. Scale checking is a core compulsive behavior in restrictive and binge-purge disorders, and daily weighing can reinforce it. Frequency here is a clinical decision, not a productivity choice - talk to your treatment team.
  • If the number reliably dictates your mood. If a 0.4 kg rise ruins a morning or triggers restriction later that day, the measurement is costing more than it's providing. Move to weekly, or stop and use how your clothes fit instead.
  • If you find yourself weighing multiple times a day. Re-weighing after using the bathroom, after a walk, before and after a meal - this is chasing noise, and it's a signal to step away from the scale rather than optimize it.
  • Children and adolescents. Routine home weighing isn't generally recommended for growing kids; growth is tracked on percentile charts by a clinician, and home scales can seed body-image problems early.
  • During pregnancy. Weight gain is expected and monitored by your midwife or doctor at appointments. Follow their schedule, not a self-tracking habit.
  • If you're training for size or strength. Muscle gain and fat loss can run simultaneously, and the scale will simply look broken. Measurements, photos and how lifts are going carry more information than weight does.

If you fall into one of these groups, you're not losing anything important. Weight is one crude proxy for health among many, and there are better proxies available for most of these situations.

Whatever the frequency, weigh the same way

Consistency does more for your data than frequency does. A daily habit under sloppy conditions is worse than a weekly one done properly.

  • Morning, after the bathroom, before eating or drinking. This is the most reproducible moment of your day.
  • Same clothing every time - or none. Clothes are a full kilogram of avoidable error.
  • Same scale, same hard floor, same spot. Carpet compresses unevenly; two scales can disagree by 1-2 kg and both be internally consistent.
  • Record it straight away, before the number has a chance to become a memory.
  • Pick a fixed weekly day if you're weighing weekly - and preferably not Monday, when a weekend's food and salt is still in transit and every week looks like a failure.

Then judge on the right timescale. Compare weekly averages, not days. Expect a healthy rate of change of roughly 0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) per week at most - see how much weight you can safely lose per week. And give a flat trend three to four weeks before you call it a stall and start working through the plateau checklist.

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad to weigh yourself every day?

For most people, no - provided you judge the weekly average and not the daily number. It's a problem if the reading changes your mood or your eating that day, if you weigh several times a day, or if you have a history of disordered eating. In those cases, weekly or less is the better choice.

What is the best time of day to weigh yourself?

First thing in the morning, after using the bathroom and before eating or drinking. It's the most reproducible moment of the day. Evening weight typically runs 1-2 kg higher simply because of food and fluid in transit - see why your weight fluctuates daily.

Should I weigh myself during maintenance?

Regular weighing is one of the habits most consistently associated with keeping weight off, because gradual regain is easy to miss otherwise. Weekly is usually plenty at that stage - enough to catch a real drift, not enough to become a preoccupation.

Why does the scale go up when I've been doing everything right?

Almost always water, glycogen and gut content rather than fat. A hard workout, a salty meal, a high-carb day or premenstrual retention can each add 0.5-2 kg for a few days. One day of genuine fat loss is around 0.07 kg (0.15 lb), which no scale can show you against that background.

How long before I should expect to see progress?

Give it three to four weeks of trend data before concluding anything. At a sensible rate of 0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) per week the first two weeks are easily hidden by normal fluctuation - see how much weight you can safely lose per week.

Health disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Self-weighing is not appropriate for everyone: if you have or have had an eating disorder, are pregnant, or are weighing a child or adolescent, follow the guidance of your doctor, midwife or treatment team rather than a general schedule. If checking the scale is causing distress, driving restriction, or becoming compulsive, please stop and speak to a qualified professional. Unintentional weight loss - particularly more than about 5% of your body weight over 6-12 months - should be discussed with a doctor.
