How Often Should You Weigh Yourself?

Last updated: August 2026

There is no single correct frequency - there's a trade-off. Daily weighing gives a far better trend; weekly gives a calmer relationship with the scale. Both are defensible, and one is clearly wrong for some people.

Here's the evidence on each side, a straight comparison of the options, and the situations where weighing more often is actively unhelpful - starting from the fact that daily fluctuation of 1-2 kg (2-4 lb) is normal.

The short answer

Actively changing your weight, and the scale doesn't upset you? Weigh daily, judge only the weekly average. Seven data points make the trend far more reliable.

The number affects your mood, or you're maintaining? Weigh once a week, same day, same conditions. Less resolution, much more peace.

Weighing has ever been part of a disordered relationship with food or your body? Weigh rarely or not at all - let a professional help you decide.

The case for weighing daily

The research is unusually consistent: people who weigh regularly do better at losing weight and at keeping it off. Why:

  • Better data. One weekly reading carries all that morning's water noise; seven averaged cancel most of it. Land the weigh-in on a salty Monday and the week's "progress" is fiction.
  • Faster feedback. A flattening trend shows up weeks earlier - adjusting in month one instead of discovering it in month three.
  • Catching regain early. Regular weighers notice a 2 kg drift and act; others notice at 7 kg.
  • Desensitization. Seeing the number bounce daily teaches you that it bounces; weekly weighers often read every wobble as meaningful.

The condition: never interpret a single day. Daily data, weekly judgement - what the weight loss chart and log template is built around.

The case for weighing weekly - and a comparison

Weekly weighing doesn't ask you to ignore anything: no daily temptation to over-read, no morning ruled by the scale. Studies find frequent weighing neutral-to-positive for mood on average - but averages hide the tails, and for some people a daily number is corrosive.

When weighing often is harmful rather than helpful

Frequent weighing is the wrong tool in several situations:

  • An eating disorder, current or past. Scale checking is a core compulsive behavior in restrictive and binge-purge disorders, and daily weighing can reinforce it. Frequency here is a clinical decision - talk to your treatment team.
  • The number dictates your mood. If a 0.4 kg rise ruins a morning or triggers restriction that day, the measurement costs more than it provides. Go weekly, or judge by how clothes fit.
  • Weighing several times a day. Before and after meals, after a walk - that's chasing noise, and a signal to step away from the scale.
  • Children and adolescents. Growth belongs on a clinician's percentile charts; routine home weighing isn't recommended and can seed body-image problems early.
  • Pregnancy. Weight gain is expected and monitored at appointments - follow your midwife or doctor's schedule.
  • Training for size or strength. Muscle gain and fat loss can run simultaneously and the scale looks broken; measurements, photos and lifts tell you more.

You lose nothing important - weight is one crude proxy for health among many.

Whatever the frequency, weigh the same way

Consistency matters more than frequency: morning, after the bathroom, before eating or drinking; same clothing or none; same scale on the same hard floor; recorded straight away. Weighing weekly? Pick a fixed day - not Monday, when the weekend's food and salt are still in transit and every week looks like a failure.

Then judge on the right timescale: weekly averages, at most 0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) per week - see how much you can safely lose per week - and three to four flat weeks before calling it a stall and opening the plateau checklist.

Notebook, spreadsheet, or app: an honest comparison

A weekly weigh-in in a notebook needs no technology, and if you'll sustain it, that's the right answer. Daily weighing is where paper breaks down: averaging seven numbers by hand every week is the chore people abandon in week three, and once averaging stops, daily data is worse than useless.

Frequently asked questions

Is it bad to weigh yourself every day?

For most people, no - provided you judge the weekly average, not the daily number. It's a problem if the reading changes your mood or eating that day, if you weigh several times a day, or if you have a history of disordered eating. Then weekly or less is the better choice.

What is the best time of day to weigh yourself?

First thing in the morning, after using the bathroom, before eating or drinking - the most reproducible moment of the day. Evening weight runs 1-2 kg higher simply from food and fluid in transit - see why your weight fluctuates daily.

Should I weigh myself during maintenance?

Regular weighing is one of the habits most consistently associated with keeping weight off, because gradual regain is easy to miss otherwise. Weekly is usually plenty at that stage - enough to catch a real drift, not enough to become a preoccupation.

Why does the scale go up when I've been doing everything right?

Almost always water, glycogen and gut content, not fat. A hard workout, a salty meal, a high-carb day or premenstrual retention can each add 0.5-2 kg for a few days - while a day of genuine fat loss is around 0.07 kg (0.15 lb), invisible against that background.

How long before I should expect to see progress?

Give it three to four weeks of trend data before concluding anything. At a sensible 0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) per week, the first two weeks are easily hidden by normal fluctuation - see how much weight you can safely lose per week.

Health disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Self-weighing is not appropriate for everyone: if you have or have had an eating disorder, are pregnant, or are weighing a child or adolescent, follow the guidance of your doctor, midwife or treatment team rather than a general schedule. If checking the scale is causing distress, driving restriction, or becoming compulsive, please stop and speak to a qualified professional. Unintentional weight loss - particularly more than about 5% of your body weight over 6-12 months - should be discussed with a doctor.
