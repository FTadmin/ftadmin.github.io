How Much Weight Can You Safely Lose per Week?

Last updated: August 2026

The standard guidance from health authorities is 0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lb) per week. It is not an arbitrary number, and it is not there to slow you down out of caution - it's the rate at which the weight you lose is mostly fat, the plan is generally sustainable, and the risks of rapid loss stay off the table.

This guide shows the arithmetic that produces that range, a table of what each rate costs you in daily calories, why the first week always beats the estimate (and why that isn't fat), and what actually goes wrong when you push harder. A useful reframing up front: for larger bodies, a better rule than a fixed number is 0.5-1% of your body weight per week, because a rate that's sensible at 120 kg is aggressive at 60 kg. And whatever rate you choose, you'll only see it in weekly averages, never in single mornings.

The deficit math

A kilogram of body fat stores roughly 7,700 calories (about 3,500 calories per pound). To lose it, you have to run that far short of what you burn. Divide by seven and you get the daily gap each rate requires:

Target rateWeekly deficitDaily deficitRealistic for
0.25 kg (0.5 lb) / week~1,900 kcal~275 kcalFine-tuning near your goal; preserving muscle
0.5 kg (1 lb) / week~3,850 kcal~550 kcalThe default sensible target for most adults
0.75 kg (1.5 lb) / week~5,750 kcal~825 kcalHigher starting weights, with good adherence
1 kg (2 lb) / week~7,700 kcal~1,100 kcalThe upper end of the standard guidance
1.5 kg (3 lb)+ / week~11,500 kcal+~1,650 kcal+Rarely achievable without medical supervision

Look at the bottom two rows and the practical problem becomes obvious. A 1,100-calorie daily deficit is roughly a third to a half of most people's total daily energy needs - achievable, but demanding. A 1,650-calorie deficit exceeds what many people burn in a day at all, which is why very rapid loss is largely the province of medically supervised programs.

One honest caveat: the 7,700-calorie rule is a useful approximation, not physics. In practice it overestimates long-run loss, because as you get lighter you burn less, so the same deficit shrinks over time. Expect real-world results to run slower than the table after the first month - that's normal, not failure, and it's the mechanism behind most weight loss plateaus.

Why the first week always beats the estimate

Almost everyone loses 2-4 kg (4-8 lb) in week one, regardless of the plan. This is genuinely encouraging and almost entirely not fat.

Cutting calories - and especially cutting carbohydrates - depletes glycogen, the carbohydrate your muscles and liver keep in reserve. Each gram of glycogen is stored with roughly three grams of water. Empty the tank and several kilograms of water leave with it. Reduced food volume in your digestive tract and lower sodium intake add to the effect.

Three consequences worth internalizing:

  • Don't extrapolate week one. Losing 3 kg in the first week does not mean 12 kg a month. Week two through four is where the true rate reveals itself.
  • Don't panic in week two. When loss drops to 0.6 kg, nothing broke. The water is gone and you're now seeing the actual fat rate.
  • A 2 kg jump after a carb-heavy weekend is the same effect running backwards. The tank refilled. It's not two kilograms of fat, which would require eating roughly 15,000 extra calories.

This is precisely why tracking the weekly average matters more than tracking days: the water noise is far bigger than the signal you're trying to read.

What goes wrong when you go faster

Faster is not simply braver. Aggressive deficits carry specific, documented costs:

  • You lose more muscle. The larger the deficit, the greater the share of the loss that comes from lean tissue rather than fat. Muscle is metabolically active, so losing it lowers your maintenance level and makes maintaining harder afterwards - the classic setup for regain.
  • Gallstones. Rapid weight loss is a well-recognized risk factor for gallstone formation, which is why supervised very-low-calorie programs monitor for it.
  • Nutrient shortfalls. Below a certain intake it becomes genuinely difficult to get enough protein, iron, calcium and B vitamins. Fatigue, hair thinning and poor recovery usually follow.
  • Deeper metabolic adaptation. Steep deficits amplify the drop in resting expenditure and the unconscious fall in everyday movement, so the deficit closes faster and the stall arrives sooner.
  • Hormonal and menstrual disruption. Prolonged severe restriction can disrupt menstrual cycles and affect bone health over time.
  • Adherence collapse and rebound. The most common outcome of all. Very restrictive plans have poor completion rates, and weight regained after a crash diet tends to come back with a worse body composition than before.

Very-low-calorie diets do have a legitimate place - typically under medical supervision, for specific clinical reasons, with monitoring. That's a treatment plan agreed with a doctor, not something to improvise from an article.

Choosing your rate

Percentages travel better than fixed numbers. A reasonable target is 0.5-1% of your current body weight per week, with the lower half of that range being the better default for most people:

Current weight0.5% / week1% / week
60 kg (132 lb)0.3 kg (0.7 lb)0.6 kg (1.3 lb)
80 kg (176 lb)0.4 kg (0.9 lb)0.8 kg (1.8 lb)
100 kg (220 lb)0.5 kg (1.1 lb)1.0 kg (2.2 lb)
120 kg (265 lb)0.6 kg (1.3 lb)1.2 kg (2.6 lb)

Three adjustments on top of that:

  • Slow down as you approach your goal. The closer you are to a healthy weight, the more of any further loss comes from muscle. The last 5 kg should be slower than the first 5 kg, not faster.
  • Protect lean mass. Adequate protein and resistance training twice a week meaningfully shift the composition of what you lose. If you're lifting, the scale will understate your progress - body fat percentage tells that story better.
  • Expect a non-linear shape. Nobody loses 0.7 kg every week for six months. Real progress looks like a staircase: two good weeks, a flat one, a small rise, then a drop. Judge it monthly.

And build in the part everyone skips: maintenance. Deliberately holding a new weight for a few months is a skill, and it's the one that determines whether any of this was worth doing.

Frequently asked questions

How much weight can you lose in a week safely?

The standard guidance is 0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lb) per week. A scalable version is 0.5-1% of your current body weight per week, which keeps the target sensible at both 60 kg and 120 kg. Larger starting weights can safely sit at the higher end; the closer you are to your goal, the slower it should be.

Is losing 2 kg a week too fast?

For most people, yes - it's roughly double the upper end of the guidance and typically means losing a larger share of muscle, with higher risks including gallstones and nutrient shortfalls. It's also usually unsustainable. Rapid loss at that pace belongs in a medically supervised program, not a self-directed plan.

Why did I lose 3 kg in my first week?

Glycogen and the water stored with it, plus less food in transit and lower sodium. It's real weight but it isn't fat, and it doesn't repeat. Your true rate shows up in weeks two to four - see why your weight fluctuates daily.

How big a calorie deficit do I need?

Roughly 550 calories a day for 0.5 kg (1 lb) per week, and about 1,100 a day for 1 kg (2 lb), based on around 7,700 calories per kilogram of fat. That estimate overshoots over time, because a lighter body burns less - expect the same deficit to produce slower loss after the first month.

Why did my weight loss slow down after a month?

Because your maintenance level fell with your weight, so the deficit you set is now smaller than it was. Everyday movement also tends to drop unconsciously during a deficit. Both are normal and both are fixable - work through how to break a weight loss plateau.

Health disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Calorie targets and rates of loss should be individualized - talk to your doctor or a registered dietitian before starting a weight loss plan, particularly if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, are under 18, have diabetes, heart, kidney or liver conditions, or take medication affecting appetite or blood sugar. Very-low-calorie diets require medical supervision. Stop and seek advice if you experience dizziness, fainting, chest pain, gallbladder pain, hair loss or menstrual changes. If dieting or tracking is causing distress or driving restrictive eating, please speak to a qualified professional.
