Weight Loss Chart and Log Template

Last updated: August 2026

A list of weights is not a weight loss chart. A chart is a list plus two things that turn it into information: context for each reading, and a weekly average that filters out the noise. Without those, you're just collecting numbers that jump around and reading each one as a verdict.

Below is a template you can copy into a notebook or spreadsheet today, a weekly summary block to go with it, and the method for pulling a real trend out of data that swings by 1-2 kg (2-4 lb) a day for reasons that have nothing to do with fat.

What to record at every weigh-in

  • Date - and keep the weigh-ins at a consistent time, ideally first thing in the morning.
  • Weight - exactly as displayed, no rounding, in whichever unit you think in. Pick kg or lbs and never switch mid-log.
  • Notes - the column that does the real work. Salty meal, hard workout, poor sleep, travel, alcohol, illness, day of cycle, first day back after a break. This is where every unexplained jump gets explained.

Three optional columns worth adding if you'll keep them up:

  • Waist circumference, measured weekly at the navel with a soft tape. It often keeps moving when the scale doesn't, and it tracks health risk better than weight alone - see waist-to-height ratio.
  • Body fat percentage, if your scale estimates it. Treat it as a rough trend, not a precise figure.
  • Daily steps, copied from your phone. Unusually useful, because everyday movement tends to fall silently during a diet.

Record immediately, before the number becomes a memory. Numbers written down an hour later drift toward what you were hoping for - a well-documented pattern in self-kept logs of every kind.

One row per weigh-in. Copy this into a notebook, or set it up as columns in a spreadsheet:

Look at that week as a whole. The daily numbers bounce between 83.7 and 84.6 - a 0.9 kg spread - and every high day has an explanation sitting next to it in the notes column. Read any single row and you'd conclude something different from reading the week. That's the whole argument for the next section.

Weigh under the same conditions every time: morning, after the bathroom, before eating or drinking, same clothing (or none), same scale on a hard floor. How often to step on it is its own decision - see how often you should weigh yourself.

The weekly summary block

This second, much smaller table is where progress actually appears. Fill in one row every Sunday evening: average the week's readings, and compare to the week before.

Four weeks, 1.5 kg down, at an average of about 0.5% of body weight per week - comfortably inside the safe range of 0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) per week. Week 3 looks like a bad week and isn't; it's a normal week with a travel note attached. Anyone reading only the daily log would have spent that week convinced everything had stopped working.

The percentage column is worth keeping. It's the number that stays meaningful as you get lighter, and it's the one that tells you whether you're going too fast.

How to read a trend from noisy data

Your daily weight is a small signal inside large noise. Water, glycogen, gut content, hormones and salt each move the scale more in a day than fat does in a week - the sizes are broken down in why your weight fluctuates daily. Reading a trend means separating those, and there are three rules that do almost all the work.

  1. Never compare two days. Compare week to week, or - better still - this week's average to the average three weeks ago. A single-day comparison is measuring water.
  2. Expect a staircase, not a slope. Real progress looks like two good weeks, a flat one, a small rise, then a drop. Weight loss is not linear for anyone, and treating a flat week as failure is the most common reason people quit a plan that was working.
  3. Use three to four flat weeks as your threshold. Below that, do nothing. At that point you have an actual signal worth acting on - work through how to break a weight loss plateau.

If you like drawing it: put date on the horizontal axis and weight on the vertical, plot the daily readings as dots, then draw a line through the middle of each week's cluster. That hand-drawn line is a trend line, and it's the thing you should be reacting to. The dots are weather; the line is climate.

One trap to avoid: do not scale the vertical axis to the last two weeks. A chart that starts at 82 kg and ends at 85 kg makes normal fluctuation look like a mountain range. Give the axis some room - a range of at least 10 kg - and the same data reads honestly.

Charting more than weight

The scale measures one thing badly and misses several things entirely. If you're training, or eating enough protein while losing, it will systematically understate your progress - muscle gained offsets fat lost, and the number sits still while your body visibly changes. Three additions fix most of that:

  • Waist measurement, weekly. Same spot, same tape, ideally in the morning. It frequently keeps falling through a scale plateau, and it maps to health risk more directly than weight does.
  • Photos, monthly. Same lighting, same position, same clothing. Nobody trusts them at the time and everybody finds them convincing three months later.
  • How clothes fit. Unglamorous, unmeasurable, and often the first honest signal of change. Write it in the notes column.

Optionally add body fat percentage if you have a way of estimating it. Home methods aren't precise, but a consistent method used consistently still shows you a direction, which is all you need.

The template above is complete. A notebook, a pen and a Sunday-evening average will run it for a year, and if that's what you'll stick with, stop reading and go rule some columns. A spreadsheet does the averaging for you and will draw the chart, which is genuinely the best free option if you enjoy spreadsheets.

Frequently asked questions

What should a weight loss chart include?

Date, weight and a notes column at minimum - the notes are what make an unexplained jump explainable. Add a weekly summary row with the week's average, the change from last week, and that change as a percentage of body weight. Waist measurement and photos are the two most valuable optional additions.

How do I work out my weekly average?

Add the week's readings and divide by how many you took - missing days are fine, just don't average a week of two readings against a week of seven. Compare averages week to week, never day to day, because daily fluctuation is far larger than a week's real progress.

How often should I update my chart?

Log every time you weigh, and update the weekly summary once a week on a fixed day. If you weigh daily you'll get the most reliable trend line; two to three times a week is a good middle ground. Avoid Monday as your summary day - the weekend's food and salt are usually still with you.

My chart is flat for two weeks. What now?

Nothing yet. Two flat weeks is well within normal fluctuation, especially after new exercise or in the second half of a menstrual cycle. Use three to four flat weeks as the threshold for acting, then work through how to break a weight loss plateau.

Should I chart in kg or pounds?

Whichever you think in - it makes no difference to the math as long as you never switch mid-log, since a change of units mid-chart makes the whole history unreadable. What's worth adding either way is the percentage-of-body-weight column, because it stays meaningful as you get lighter.
