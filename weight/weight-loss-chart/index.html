Weight Loss Chart and Log Template

Last updated: August 2026

A list of weights is not a chart. A chart is a list plus two things: context for each reading, and a weekly average that filters the noise. Without those, you're reading each number as a verdict.

Below: a template to copy, a weekly summary block, and how to pull a real trend from data that swings 1-2 kg (2-4 lb) a day for reasons unrelated to fat.

What to record at every weigh-in

  • Date - weigh at a consistent time, ideally first thing.
  • Weight - exactly as displayed, no rounding. Pick kg or lbs; never switch mid-log.
  • Notes - the column that does the real work: salty meal, hard workout, poor sleep, travel, alcohol, illness, day of cycle. Where every unexplained jump gets explained.

Worth adding: weekly waist circumference - it often keeps moving when the scale doesn't (see waist-to-height ratio) - and daily steps, because everyday movement falls silently during a diet.

Record immediately: numbers written an hour later drift toward what you hoped for.

Four weeks, 1.5 kg down, about 0.5% of body weight per week - inside the safe range of 0.5-1 kg (1-2 lb) per week. Week 3 looks bad and isn't - it's a normal week with a travel note. Anyone reading only the daily log spent it convinced the plan had stopped.

Keep the percentage column - it stays meaningful as you lighten and says whether you're going too fast.

How to read a trend from noisy data

Water, glycogen, gut content, hormones and salt each move the scale more in a day than fat does in a week (sizes in why your weight fluctuates daily). Three rules do the work:

  1. Never compare two days - compare week to week, or this week's average to three weeks ago; single days measure water.
  2. Expect a staircase, not a slope. Two good weeks, a flat one, a small rise, a drop. Treating the flat week as failure is why people quit working plans.
  3. Use three to four flat weeks as your threshold. Below that, do nothing; past it, work through how to break a weight loss plateau.

Drawing by hand? Readings as dots, a line through each week's cluster - dots are weather, the line is climate. Don't scale the axis to the last two weeks: give it 10 kg, or a chart from 82 to 85 kg turns fluctuation into a mountain range.

Charting more than weight

The scale measures one thing badly and misses several entirely: if you're training, muscle gained offsets fat lost while your body visibly changes. Three additions fix that:

  • Waist, weekly. Same spot, same tape, morning. It frequently keeps falling through a scale plateau.
  • Photos, monthly. Same lighting, position, clothing. Nobody trusts them now; everybody finds them convincing three months later.
  • How clothes fit. Unmeasurable, and often the first honest signal. Write it in the notes.

Optionally add body fat percentage: home methods aren't precise, but a consistent one shows a direction.

Frequently asked questions

What should a weight loss chart include?

Date, weight and a notes column at minimum - the notes make an unexplained jump explainable. Add a weekly summary row with the week's average, the change from last week, and that change as a percentage of body weight. Waist measurement and photos are the most valuable optional additions.

How do I work out my weekly average?

Add the week's readings and divide by how many you took - missing days are fine, just don't average a two-reading week against a seven-reading one. Compare averages week to week, never day to day, because daily fluctuation is far larger than a week's real progress.

How often should I update my chart?

Log every time you weigh, and update the weekly summary once a week on a fixed day. Daily weighing gives the most reliable trend line; two to three times a week is a good middle ground. Avoid Monday as your summary day - the weekend's food and salt are usually still with you.

My chart is flat for two weeks. What now?

Nothing yet. Two flat weeks is well within normal fluctuation, especially after new exercise or in the second half of a menstrual cycle. Use three to four flat weeks as the threshold, then work through how to break a weight loss plateau.

Should I chart in kg or pounds?

Whichever you think in - just never switch mid-log, since a change of units makes the whole history unreadable. Either way, add the percentage-of-body-weight column: it stays meaningful as you get lighter.
