Healthy Weight for Your Height

Last updated: August 2026

There is no single correct weight for a given height — there is a range, and it is wider than most people expect. At 170 cm, roughly 5 ft 7 in, the healthy band spans about 19 kg or 42 pounds. Two people at opposite ends of it can both be perfectly healthy, because they are carrying different amounts of muscle, bone, and fat.

The tables below give that range for every height, in both metric and imperial units. They are derived from the standard BMI categories, which put the healthy band at a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9. After the tables, the more useful question: how to pick your own number inside that range, and when the range does not apply to you at all.

Adults are commonly a centimeter or two shorter than they believe, and height does slowly decrease from middle age onward.

Why the healthy range is so wide

A 42-pound band looks imprecise until you consider what varies between two people of identical height.

  • Muscle mass. The single biggest factor. Muscle is denser than fat, so a physically strong person legitimately weighs more at the same body size. Someone who lifts three times a week may sit near the top of the band, or above it, with less body fat than someone sitting in the middle.
  • Frame size. Bone width and shoulder breadth vary meaningfully between people. There is no need to measure it precisely — it simply means the range has to accommodate both narrow and broad builds.
  • Sex. Women carry more essential body fat than men at every height, but the tables above make no distinction. In practice, women often sit healthily lower in the band and men higher.
  • Age. Body composition shifts with age even at a stable weight. For older adults, the very bottom of the range is often not the healthiest place to be — low weight in later life is associated with frailty and poorer recovery from illness.
  • Ancestry. Several health bodies apply lower thresholds for people of South Asian and East Asian descent, where metabolic risk starts to rise at a lower BMI. If that applies to you, the top of the tables above may be higher than your doctor would advise.

These are also the reasons a weight inside the range is not automatic proof of health, and a weight slightly outside it is not automatic cause for concern.

Finding your own number inside the range

Rather than aiming at a specific figure from a table, use these to narrow it down.

  1. Check your waist. The most useful single addition to the tables. Your waist measurement should be less than half your height — a check that takes thirty seconds and directly reflects the fat distribution that matters most. See waist-to-height ratio.
  2. Look at where you have been stable. If you maintained a weight comfortably for a year or more without restricting, that is strong evidence it is a sustainable weight for your body. Bodies defend a familiar weight far more easily than an aspirational one.
  3. Consider composition, not just mass. If you are training, weight can stay flat while the mirror and your clothes change. Body fat percentage catches what the scale hides.
  4. Ask what your doctor is actually watching. Blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, and how you feel day to day are the outcomes weight is a proxy for. A body that scores well on those is doing its job.
  5. If you want to move, move slowly. A target within about 5 to 10 percent of your current weight is the one most people reach and keep. The pacing that works is in how much weight you can safely lose per week.

And if you are currently well outside the range, the first target is not the top of the healthy band. Losing 5 percent of your body weight already produces measurable improvements in blood pressure and blood sugar. That is a real destination, reachable this year.

Keeping track: notebook vs app

Knowing your target range is the easy part. The awkward part is that weight moves day to day for reasons unrelated to fat — food still in transit, hydration, salt, hormones — so a single reading rarely tells you whether you are moving toward the range or away from it. Only the trend does.

A notebook handles this perfectly well if you are disciplined: same time each morning, one line per day, and a mental average across the week. Plenty of people have reached and held a healthy weight with exactly that. What it will not do is draw you a trend line, and the trend line is the entire point.

Frequently asked questions

What is a healthy weight for a 5 ft 6 in woman?

Roughly 115 to 154 lb, or 52 to 70 kg. The tables are not sex-specific, so women often sit healthily in the lower half of that band — but muscle, frame, and body composition all shift where an individual belongs within it.

Is there an ideal weight for my height?

Not a single number, no. Older formulas produced one figure per height, but they were built for medication dosing rather than health, and they ignore muscle entirely. A range is more honest — and where you sit inside it matters less than your waist measurement, activity, and blood markers.

Should men and women use different weight-for-height tables?

The standard tables are the same for both, because they come straight from BMI, which does not distinguish by sex. If you want a sex-specific measure, body fat percentage has separate healthy ranges for men and women.

What if I'm above the range but very muscular?

Then the table is probably wrong about you. Weight scaled to height cannot tell muscle from fat, and trained people routinely exceed the healthy band while being lean. Check your waist against half your height, and use the mirror and your strength as the real measures.

How long should it take to reach a healthy weight?

Longer than most plans suggest, and that is fine. About 0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lb) per week is the range most guidance considers sustainable — see safe weight loss rate. Reaching a 10 kg goal in five months and keeping it beats losing it in six weeks and regaining it.

Health disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Healthy weight ranges are population guidance and cannot account for your individual build, health conditions, or medications — always discuss weight targets and any plan to change your weight with your doctor or healthcare provider. Seek medical advice promptly if you are losing or gaining weight without trying, or if weight change comes with symptoms such as persistent fatigue, breathlessness, or loss of appetite.
