BMI Chart for Adults

Last updated: August 2026

Body mass index is a single number that compares your weight to your height. It takes two seconds to look up on a chart and it is the first screening figure almost every clinician reaches for — not because it is precise, but because it is quick, free, and roughly predictive across large groups of people.

Below is the full adult BMI chart, a height-and-weight table so you can find your range without any math, and an honest account of what the number does and does not tell you about your health. If you would rather work it out yourself, see how to calculate BMI.

The adult BMI chart

These categories apply to adults aged 20 and over, of any sex. They are the World Health Organization thresholds, used more or less unchanged by health services worldwide.

CategoryBMI rangeWhat it generally indicates
UnderweightBelow 18.5May signal undernutrition, illness, or simply a naturally light frame
Healthy weight18.5 – 24.9The range associated with the lowest average health risk
Overweight25.0 – 29.9Slightly raised average risk; body composition matters a lot here
Obesity class 130.0 – 34.9Raised risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, joint problems
Obesity class 235.0 – 39.9Substantially raised risk; worth a conversation with a doctor
Obesity class 340.0 and aboveHighest risk category; medical support is usually recommended

Two important footnotes. First, the boundaries are conventions, not cliffs — 24.9 and 25.1 are the same body. Second, several health bodies use lower thresholds for people of South Asian, Chinese, and other Asian ancestry, where the risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease rises at a lower BMI: overweight from about 23 and obesity from about 27.5. Ask your doctor which set of cutoffs applies to you.

There is no single correct weight for a given height, only a range within which most people do well — which is also why a healthy weight for your height is better thought of as a target zone than a target number.

What your category actually means

A BMI category is a starting point for a conversation, not a verdict. Here is the honest reading of each one.

  • Underweight. Worth investigating rather than celebrating. Unintentional weight loss, low appetite, or a BMI that keeps drifting down deserves a doctor's attention — it can point to thyroid problems, malabsorption, or an eating disorder. Some people are simply built light and perfectly healthy.
  • Healthy weight. Statistically the lowest-risk band, but it is not a guarantee. Someone with very little muscle and a lot of abdominal fat can sit at BMI 23 and still carry meaningful metabolic risk — sometimes called normal-weight obesity.
  • Overweight. The category where BMI is least informative for an individual. A muscular person and a sedentary person can share a BMI of 27 with completely different health profiles. This is where waist-to-height ratio earns its keep.
  • Obesity classes 1 to 3. As BMI rises, the odds of misclassifying a healthy body fall sharply, because very few people carry enough muscle to reach BMI 35 without excess fat. Risk of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnea, and joint pain rises with each class.

Across every category, the direction of travel matters more than the snapshot. A BMI drifting up 0.5 points a year is a more useful signal than any single reading, and it is the thing a chart alone can never show you.

Where the BMI chart falls short

BMI was devised in the 19th century to describe populations, not to assess individuals. It divides weight by height squared and nothing else — so it cannot see any of the following.

  • Muscle versus fat. Muscle is denser than fat. Rugby players, sprinters, and serious lifters routinely land in the overweight or obese categories with single-digit body fat.
  • Where the fat sits. Fat around the organs is far more metabolically harmful than fat on the hips and thighs, and BMI treats both identically.
  • Age. Adults lose muscle and gain fat with age at a steady BMI, so an older adult at BMI 24 may carry noticeably more fat than they did at 30.
  • Sex. Women naturally carry a higher proportion of body fat than men at the same BMI, and the chart makes no distinction.
  • Ancestry. Health risk begins at different BMI values in different populations, which is why several countries publish adjusted cutoffs.
  • Special cases. BMI is not valid during pregnancy, and children and teenagers are assessed with age-and-sex percentile charts instead of these adult categories.

None of this makes BMI useless — it makes it one input. Pair it with a tape measure and, if you want the fullest picture, an estimate of your body fat percentage. Two numbers that disagree tell you more than one number that seems definitive.

Tracking BMI over time: notebook vs app

Looking your BMI up once is useful. Watching it move over months is far more useful, because that is the part a chart on a webpage cannot do. A notebook works fine for this: weigh yourself at the same time of day, write down the number, and recalculate BMI every few weeks. Plenty of people have done exactly that for years.

The friction shows up later. Daily weight bounces around by a kilogram or two for reasons that have nothing to do with fat, so a column of handwritten numbers is genuinely hard to read — the signal is a trend line, and trend lines are not something anyone draws by hand twice. Frequently asked questions

What is a good BMI for an adult?

Anything from 18.5 to 24.9 is classified as a healthy weight, and there is no meaningful difference in risk across that range. Rather than aiming for a specific figure, aim to sit comfortably inside the band for your height — see the table in healthy weight for your height.

Is the BMI chart different for men and women?

No. The adult categories are identical for both, even though women naturally carry a higher percentage of body fat at the same BMI. That is one of the chart's known limitations, and one reason body fat percentage uses separate ranges for men and women.

Does the BMI chart work for children?

No. Children and teenagers are assessed using age- and sex-specific percentile charts, because healthy body composition changes throughout growth. A child's BMI is compared to other children of the same age rather than to the adult thresholds above.

Can you be healthy with a BMI over 25?

Yes, and many people are — particularly if the extra weight is muscle, your waist measurement is under half your height, and your blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol are all normal. BMI is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. Those other markers are what your doctor will actually act on.

How often should I check my BMI?

Monthly is plenty for most people. BMI only moves when weight moves meaningfully, and checking daily just exposes you to normal day-to-day fluctuation that has nothing to do with body composition.

Health disclaimer

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. BMI is a screening tool and cannot assess an individual's health on its own — always discuss your weight, and any plan to change it, with your doctor or healthcare provider. Seek medical advice promptly if you are losing or gaining weight without trying, or if weight change comes with symptoms such as persistent fatigue, breathlessness, or loss of appetite.
