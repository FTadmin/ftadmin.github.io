How to Calculate BMI

Last updated: August 2026

Body mass index is your weight divided by your height squared. That is the whole idea — one division and one multiplication, which is why the number has survived nearly two hundred years and appears on every clinic wall. You can do it on a phone keypad in about ten seconds.

This guide gives you both versions of the formula, metric and imperial, worked through with real numbers. Then it covers the part most BMI calculators skip: the specific situations where the number you just worked out will mislead you, and what to look at instead. Once you have your figure, the BMI chart tells you which category it falls into.

The metric formula: kilograms and meters

If you already think in kilograms and centimeters, this is the easy one.

BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in meters × height in meters)

The only trap is the height unit. It is meters, not centimeters — 175 cm is 1.75 m. Using 175 gives you a BMI of about 0.0025, which is a good sign you have skipped the conversion.

Worked example

Someone who weighs 78 kg and stands 175 cm tall:

  1. Convert height to meters: 175 cm = 1.75 m
  2. Square it: 1.75 × 1.75 = 3.0625
  3. Divide weight by that: 78 ÷ 3.0625 = 25.5

A BMI of 25.5 sits just inside the overweight category, which begins at 25.0. Notice how narrow the margin is: this person is roughly 1.5 kg from the healthy band. Never treat a result that lands within a point of a boundary as a firm classification — normal daily weight fluctuation alone can move you across the line and back within a week.

The imperial formula: pounds and inches

If you weigh in pounds and measure your height in feet and inches, the formula picks up a conversion factor.

BMI = (weight in pounds × 703) ÷ (height in inches × height in inches)

That 703 is not arbitrary. It converts pounds per square inch into kilograms per square meter: there are 39.37 inches in a meter and 2.2046 pounds in a kilogram, and 39.37 squared divided by 2.2046 comes to roughly 703. Use it and you get exactly the same answer as the metric version.

Worked example

Someone who weighs 172 lb and stands 5 ft 9 in tall:

  1. Convert height entirely to inches: (5 × 12) + 9 = 69 in
  2. Square it: 69 × 69 = 4,761
  3. Multiply weight by 703: 172 × 703 = 120,916
  4. Divide: 120,916 ÷ 4,761 = 25.4

Same person as the metric example, near enough — 172 lb is about 78 kg and 5 ft 9 in is about 175 cm — and the same answer, as it should be. The most common imperial mistake is leaving the feet and inches separate and squaring only the inches.

Mistakes that quietly change your answer

The arithmetic is simple, so most wrong BMIs come from the inputs rather than the math.

  • Guessing your height. Most adults are a centimeter or two shorter than the number on their driver's license, and height genuinely shrinks with age. At 175 cm, being 1 cm out changes BMI by about 0.3 — enough to move you across a category boundary. Measure barefoot, heels against a wall, once, and reuse that figure.
  • Weighing at different times of day. You can be 1 to 2 kg heavier in the evening than first thing in the morning, purely from food and fluid. Weigh at the same time, ideally after waking and using the bathroom, before eating or drinking.
  • Mixing unit systems. Kilograms with inches, or pounds with meters, produces a nonsense figure. Commit to one system and convert first.
  • Squaring the weight instead of the height. It happens more often than you would think when typing quickly.
  • Over-rounding. Rounding height to the nearest 5 cm or weight to the nearest 5 kg can shift the result by a full point.

If two calculators give you slightly different answers, this is almost always why — one is rounding your height, or you entered it differently.

The honest limitations of BMI

BMI does exactly one thing: it scales weight for height. Everything else about your body is invisible to it. That matters most in these cases.

  • Athletes and muscular people. Muscle is denser than fat, so it costs BMI points without costing health. A well-trained rower or rugby forward can post a BMI of 28 to 30 at a body fat percentage most people would envy. If you lift seriously or play a contact sport, treat your BMI as close to meaningless and use body fat percentage or a tape measure instead.
  • The reverse case. Someone sedentary with little muscle can sit at BMI 23 while carrying enough abdominal fat to raise their metabolic risk. A healthy BMI is not evidence of a healthy body composition.
  • Ethnicity-specific cutoffs. The standard thresholds were derived largely from European populations. Risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease rises at a lower BMI in South Asian and East Asian populations, and some evidence suggests the opposite adjustment for some Black populations. Ask your doctor which set applies to you rather than assuming the default.
  • Age. Adults gradually swap muscle for fat at a constant weight, so the same BMI at 60 usually represents more body fat than it did at 30. Very low BMI in older adults is also its own risk, not a safety margin.
  • Sex. Women carry more essential body fat than men at any given BMI, yet the categories make no distinction.
  • Pregnancy, children, and very tall or short people. BMI is not valid in pregnancy. Children and teenagers use age- and sex-based percentile charts. And because BMI divides by height squared rather than cubed, it runs slightly high for tall people and slightly low for short people.

The cheapest fix for all of this is a tape measure. Waist-to-height ratio takes thirty seconds, needs no scale, and directly measures the fat distribution BMI cannot see. Used together, the two numbers rarely both mislead you.

Frequently asked questions

What is the BMI formula?

In metric: weight in kg ÷ (height in m)². In imperial: (weight in lb × 703) ÷ (height in in)². Both give the same result — the 703 simply converts pounds and inches into the metric equivalent.

Why is 703 used in the imperial BMI formula?

It is the unit conversion baked into the equation. One meter is 39.37 inches and one kilogram is 2.2046 pounds, so 39.37² ÷ 2.2046 comes out at roughly 703. Multiplying by it turns pounds per square inch into kilograms per square meter.

Is BMI calculated differently for men and women?

No — the formula and the adult categories are identical. That is a known weakness rather than a design choice, since women carry more essential body fat at the same BMI. If you want a sex-specific measure, use body fat percentage, which has separate ranges.

How accurate is BMI for athletes?

Poor. Muscle weighs more than fat for the same volume, so trained athletes routinely land in the overweight or obese categories while being extremely lean. For anyone doing serious resistance training, waist measurement and body fat estimates are far more informative.

Do I need to recalculate BMI every time I weigh myself?

Not manually. Your height rarely changes, so BMI moves in lockstep with weight — if your weight is trending down, so is your BMI. Checking the figure monthly is plenty, and an app recalculates it automatically anyway.
