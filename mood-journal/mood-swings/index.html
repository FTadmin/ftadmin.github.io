Mood Swings: Causes and Patterns

Last updated: August 2026

Everyone's mood moves. It moves with sleep, with hunger, with the hour of the day, with who you spent the afternoon around. "Mood swings" is the phrase people reach for when that movement feels bigger, faster, or less explainable than they'd like — when the day turns on something small and the turn seems to come from nowhere.

Most of the time it isn't from nowhere. It's from something that happened six hours earlier, or the night before, or on a cycle too slow to notice from the inside. This guide covers the everyday contributors worth ruling in or out, how to find your own pattern with a few weeks of simple tracking, and how to tell when a pattern is worth raising with a professional.

What counts as a mood swing

There's no clinical threshold where ordinary variation becomes a "swing," which is part of why the phrase causes so much unnecessary worry. It's more useful to describe what's actually happening on four axes:

  • Size. How far you travel. On a 5-point scale, a one-point move across a day is unremarkable. Repeatedly covering three or four points is a bigger range than most people's.
  • Speed. A gradual slope over a week feels different from a drop within an hour, even when the endpoints match.
  • Duration. Minutes, hours, days, or weeks at a time. A brief flare and a fortnight in the low band are different things.
  • Fit. Whether the shift makes sense given what happened. Feeling awful after awful news is your emotions working correctly.

Those four are worth writing down for yourself, because "I get mood swings" is too vague to act on, whereas "I drop two points within an hour, several times a week, usually late afternoon" is almost a solved problem — it points straight at food, caffeine, blood sugar and the end of the working day.

These are the everyday drivers worth ruling in or out before reaching for a bigger explanation. Most people who track for a month find that two or three of them account for a surprising share of their variation.

Notice how many of these have a lag. That's the main reason mood swings feel causeless: the trigger has usually left the room by the time the mood arrives, so the mind attaches the feeling to whatever is happening now. A dated log fixes this better than memory can.

Finding your own pattern

The method is unglamorous and it works. Log a rating once or twice a day, tag two or three suspects, and after three or four weeks read the record rather than your impressions of it. The mood chart guide covers the reading in detail; three things matter most when you're specifically chasing swings:

  1. Log at the shift, not just at bedtime. A single evening entry averages the swing away. If the interesting thing is the change, catch it near the change and note the hour.
  2. Write down what was happening in the hour before. One line. Not analysis — just the raw material: where you were, who with, what you'd eaten, how you slept.
  3. Check yesterday's column, not just today's. Sleep, alcohol and caffeine typically show up a day late. Comparing same-day columns is how people conclude, wrongly, that nothing affects them.

Two habits protect you from finding patterns that aren't there. Count the exceptions — a suspected trigger that holds only half the time is a coincidence with a good story. And change one thing at a time for a fortnight rather than overhauling everything at once, or you'll never know which change did the work.

Sometimes the difficulty isn't the pattern but the label: "bad" covers anger, dread, disappointment and exhaustion, which have completely different triggers. Naming the feeling more precisely — the emotion wheel is a good tool for this — often makes the pattern obvious.

What helps in the moment

Understanding a pattern is a medium-term project. In the middle of a swing, the goal is smaller: get through it without doing something you'll have to repair afterwards.

  • Check the basics first. Water, food, a short walk, fresh air. It's faintly insulting how often one of these is the whole answer, and it costs five minutes to rule out.
  • Put time between feeling and acting. The intensity of most emotional peaks falls substantially within twenty to thirty minutes if you don't feed them. Delaying the message, the reply, the decision is often the entire skill.
  • Come back into the body. Slow breathing with a longer exhale, cold water on the wrists, or a sensory exercise — the grounding techniques guide has several that take under a minute.
  • Name it. Saying "this is anxiety, and it will pass" is not positive thinking; putting words to a feeling reliably takes some of the heat out of it.
  • Log it afterwards. Thirty seconds while it's fresh. Today's entry is what makes next month's pattern visible.

Structured skills for exactly this — riding out intensity without making things worse — are the core of DBT, and the plain-English guide to DBT skills covers the ones most people find useful first.

When a pattern is worth raising with a professional

Mood swings are common and usually explainable, and this section is not a checklist for diagnosing yourself — no article can do that, and self-diagnosis from a symptom list tends to produce more distress than clarity. What a few weeks of tracking can do is tell you when it's time to have a conversation. It's worth booking one if you recognize any of these:

  • Low mood most of the day, most days, for more than two weeks, or a lasting loss of interest in things you normally enjoy.
  • The swings are getting in the way of work, study, sleep, or relationships you care about.
  • Periods of unusually elevated mood or energy with much less need for sleep, fast speech or racing thoughts, or decisions that look out of character afterwards.
  • Shifts that are very fast and very intense, often around perceived rejection or conflict, alongside a longstanding sense of unstable self-image or relationships. The guide on BPD and mood tracking covers what people usually want to know here.
  • You're using alcohol or other substances to manage the swings.
  • Anything that arrived alongside a new or changed medication — worth a call to your prescriber rather than a decision on your own.
  • Any thoughts of harming yourself.

That last one deserves its own line. If you're having thoughts of self-harm or suicide, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline in your country now. You don't need to wait until it feels serious enough, and you don't have to be certain about anything to ask for help.

For everything else, bring your log. "I've been up and down" is hard for a clinician to work with; six weeks of dated entries showing a two-point daily range, clustered after short nights, is something you can actually look at together — and it's often the fastest way to get to a useful answer.

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

A notebook by the bed captures all of this. If you'll write in it during a hard hour, it's genuinely enough, and there's something to be said for a record that doesn't live inside the device most of your triggers arrive on.

Where paper struggles is the moment that matters most for swings: catching an entry at the shift itself, when you're on a train or standing outside a meeting room. And a notebook can't line six weeks of ratings up against six weeks of sleep tags. An app takes the boring half:

  • An entry in about five seconds — a tap on the 5-point scale plus a factor tag, which is short enough to happen during the swing rather than after it.
  • Factors tagged per entry, then charted against mood, so "does caffeine actually do this to me?" takes seconds to check instead of an evening with a spreadsheet.
  • Notes and photos when a line of text isn't enough to bring the moment back.
  • Reminders and widgets, so the habit survives the weeks you most need the data.
  • Apple Health sync to pull in sleep instead of typing it, since sleep is the contributor most worth having accurate.
  • iCloud sync with no account and no email — this is private material, and it stays on your devices.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON for the appointment where a clear record does more than a summary from memory.
  • AI insights over your own history, free with an optional premium tier, offering a second read on what your entries show — observations about your data, not advice, and not a clinician.

Either way, the tool only observes. A mood journal supports DBT-style self-monitoring and makes professional conversations more concrete; it doesn't treat or diagnose anything. The record you'll actually keep is the one worth having.

Frequently asked questions

Are mood swings normal?

Everyday variation is completely normal — mood moves with sleep, food, hormones, stress and the hour of the day. What's worth looking at more closely is persistence and impact: swings that last for weeks, keep getting in the way of work or relationships, or feel impossible to ride out. Frequency alone isn't the measure.

Why do my mood swings seem to come from nowhere?

Usually because the trigger arrived earlier than the mood. Sleep, alcohol and caffeine typically show up a day late, and hormonal patterns run on a monthly cycle you can't feel from inside a single day. A dated log with factor tags is much better at spotting lagged causes than memory is — see how to track your mood.

How fast should a mood swing pass?

There's no correct speed, but the peak of an emotional surge often eases substantially within twenty to thirty minutes if it isn't fed by rumination or an argument. Putting time between the feeling and any action is the practical skill — the grounding techniques guide covers ways to do that.

Could this be hormonal?

Quite possibly, and tracking is the way to find out. Log a daily rating plus your cycle day for two to three months — a pattern that repeats with the same shape each month is far more convincing than a single difficult week. Bring that record to a doctor if the pattern is disruptive.

When should I see a doctor about mood swings?

If low mood lasts most of the day for more than two weeks, if the swings are interfering with daily life, if there are periods of unusually high energy with little need for sleep, or if anything started after a medication change. If you're having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline right away.
