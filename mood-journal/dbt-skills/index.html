DBT Skills: A Plain-English Guide

Last updated: August 2026

Dialectical behavior therapy — DBT — is a structured, skills-based therapy originally developed for people who experience emotions that arrive fast and land hard. Its reputation comes from something unusually practical: rather than only talking about feelings, it teaches a named set of skills you can reach for at 2am, when everything feels like too much and good intentions are not enough.

This guide walks through all four DBT modules in ordinary language, with one concrete skill from each that you can try this week. It is an explainer, not a course. Real DBT is delivered by trained therapists, usually as a package of individual sessions and a weekly skills group — what you can do on your own is learn the vocabulary, practice the simpler skills, and start noticing which ones actually help you. That last part is what a daily mood tracking habit is for.

What DBT is — and what it isn't

DBT was developed by psychologist Marsha Linehan in the 1980s, initially for people living with intense emotional swings and self-harm urges, and it is now used far more widely — for borderline personality disorder, eating disorders, substance use, chronic suicidality, and by plenty of people who simply find their emotions run hotter than they'd like.

The word dialectical points at the idea holding the whole thing together: two opposite things can be true at once. You are doing the best you can with what you have, and you can learn to do things differently. Acceptance and change, side by side, rather than one at the expense of the other. Every module is built on that balance.

Standard, full-program DBT has four parts, and it's worth knowing what they are so you know what you're comparing against:

  • Individual therapy — weekly, one-to-one, working through what happened and what got in the way.
  • Skills group — a class, essentially, that cycles through the four modules over several months.
  • Phone coaching — brief between-session contact for applying a skill in the moment it's needed.
  • A consultation team for the therapists, which is why the model holds up.

So here is the honest framing for a page like this one: learning the skills from an article is genuinely useful, and it is not the same thing as doing DBT. If your emotions regularly overwhelm you, a referral to a trained DBT therapist is the step that changes things. Everything below is a map, not the territory.

The four modules at a glance

Two of the modules are about accepting reality as it is (mindfulness and distress tolerance) and two are about changing it (emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness). Mindfulness sits underneath all of them — it's taught first and revisited at the start of every cycle, because none of the other skills work if you can't notice what's happening in time to use them.

Distress tolerance skills are the emergency kit — they don't solve anything, and they're not meant to. Their whole job is to get you to the other side of a bad hour intact so that the change-oriented skills have something to work with tomorrow.

Four skills, in detail

Wise mind (mindfulness)

DBT describes three states: emotion mind, where feelings drive the decision; reasonable mind, which is all facts and no warmth; and wise mind, the overlap where you know something calmly and completely. The practice is simple to describe and takes a while to get: pause, breathe, ask the question you're stuck on, and wait for the answer that feels settled rather than urgent. Urgency is usually emotion mind talking.

TIPP (distress tolerance)

For the moments when distress is at a nine or ten and no amount of reasoning is going to land. TIPP works on your physiology instead of your thoughts:

  • T — Temperature. Cold water on the face, or a cold pack over the eyes and cheeks, held while you hold your breath briefly. This triggers the body's dive reflex, which slows the heart rate. It works quickly and it feels strange the first time.
  • I — Intense exercise. A short, hard burst — running up the stairs, star jumps, a brisk walk pushed to the point of breathlessness. Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty.
  • P — Paced breathing. Slow the breath down and make the out-breath longer than the in-breath (in for four, out for six or eight).
  • P — Paired muscle relaxation. Tense a muscle group on the in-breath, release it on the out-breath, and work through the body.

One important caveat: the cold-water and intense-exercise skills change heart rate deliberately, so check with your doctor before using them if you have a heart condition, an eating disorder, are pregnant, or take medication that affects your heart rate. The breathing and muscle-relaxation halves are gentle and suit almost everyone. Several of these overlap with the wider set of grounding techniques.

Opposite action (emotion regulation)

Every emotion comes with an action urge: fear says avoid, shame says hide, anger says attack, sadness says withdraw. Sometimes the urge fits the facts and following it is right. Often it doesn't — the fear is out of proportion, or the shame belongs to something that wasn't your fault — and following it makes the emotion stronger next time.

Opposite action means checking the facts first, then, if the urge doesn't fit, doing the opposite all the way: send the message you're afraid to send, and send it warmly. Stay in the room instead of leaving it. Stand up straight and speak gently instead of hiding. Half-hearted opposite action doesn't work; the completeness is the active ingredient.

DEAR MAN (interpersonal effectiveness)

A script for asking for something, or refusing something, when the conversation matters: Describe the situation factually, Express how you feel about it, Assert what you want clearly, Reinforce by naming what the other person gets out of it — then stay Mindful of your goal when the conversation drifts, Appear confident, and Negotiate where you can. Its two companions cover the other priorities: GIVE for keeping the relationship intact, FAST for keeping your self-respect.

The diary card: DBT's own tracking habit

There's a piece of DBT that rarely makes it into articles about skills, and it's arguably the piece that makes the skills stick: the diary card. DBT clients fill one in daily — emotions rated, urges noted, which skills were used and whether they helped — and the card is what the individual session opens with. The therapist reads the week rather than asking "so, how have you been?" and receiving whatever the last two days happen to have left in memory.

You don't need to be in a DBT program to borrow it. A workable minimal version, one entry a day:

  • A rating for how the day felt overall — a simple scale is enough, and consistency beats precision.
  • The factors in play: sleep, alcohol, a conflict, a deadline, physical illness, hormones, isolation.
  • Any urges you noticed and whether you acted on them.
  • Which skill you tried, and — the field everyone skips — whether it actually helped.

After a month, that last column is the useful one. Almost nobody's helpful-skills list matches what they expected: people who assumed breathing exercises were useless discover paced breathing works for them, and people who love the idea of journaling find it makes rumination worse at night. Tracking is how you find your own list instead of the generic one. If you'd rather start from feelings than from ratings, the emotion wheel is a good companion for naming what you're actually feeling before you rate it.

Paper diary card vs an app: an honest comparison

The classic diary card is a printed sheet, and there is a real argument for it. It's free, it works when your phone is out of reach, therapists often hand you one, and writing by hand slows you down in a way that suits reflection. If you're in a program and your therapist wants the paper card, use the paper card.

What paper is bad at is the second month. A stack of loose sheets doesn't add up to anything you can look at; nobody counts how many Tuesdays were rough; and the card is never in your pocket at the moment the urge shows up. That's the gap an app closes:

  • Logging takes about five seconds — a five-point scale plus the factors you tag, which is short enough to survive a bad week.
  • Charts and trends turn a hundred entries into a shape you can see, including which factors travel with your worst days.
  • Reminders, so the daily card actually happens rather than getting reconstructed on Sunday night.
  • Notes and photos for the context a rating can't hold, and free-text room to write out a DEAR MAN before you send it.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON — the whole point when your next session is on Thursday and you'd rather hand over a chart than a memory.
  • Private by design: iCloud sync with no account, no email address, no login, and Apple Health sync if you want sleep and activity next to your ratings.

Frequently asked questions

Can I learn DBT skills on my own?

You can learn and practice many of them — the skills are taught openly, and workbooks exist for exactly this reason. What you can't self-provide is the rest of the program: a therapist who knows your history, a group that practices with you, and coaching in the moment. Treat solo learning as a genuinely useful start, not a substitute for care if your emotions regularly overwhelm you.

What's the difference between DBT and CBT?

DBT grew out of CBT and shares its practical, skills-first approach. The difference is emphasis: CBT concentrates on examining and changing unhelpful thoughts, while DBT adds a large acceptance half — tolerating distress, mindfulness, and validating that your reaction makes sense — alongside the change work. DBT is also more structured, with a defined skills curriculum and a group component.

Do I need a BPD diagnosis to use DBT skills?

No. DBT was developed with borderline personality disorder in mind and is well established there, but the skills themselves are general-purpose emotional tools and are now used for many other difficulties. Plenty of people with no diagnosis at all find TIPP and opposite action useful. If you're reading up on this because something feels familiar, our guide to BPD and mood tracking is a gentler place to start than a symptom checklist.

How long does a full DBT program take?

A standard skills group runs through the four modules over roughly six months, and many programs recommend two full cycles — so about a year, alongside weekly individual therapy. Shorter, skills-only groups are increasingly common. Your clinician will tell you what's realistic where you live.

What should I track between sessions?

The traditional diary card: a daily mood rating, the factors around it, any urges, and which skills you used and whether they helped. Keep it small enough that you'll still do it on a bad day — see how to track your mood for a version that takes about five seconds.
