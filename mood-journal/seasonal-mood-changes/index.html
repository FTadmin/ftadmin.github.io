Seasonal Mood Changes

Last updated: August 2026

Most people feel some difference between a bright June and a dark January. For a smaller group the change is much larger — energy drains away in autumn, everything takes more effort through the winter, and then spring arrives and it lifts, reliably, year after year. When that pattern is strong enough to interfere with daily life, clinicians describe it as seasonal affective disorder, or more precisely as depression with a seasonal pattern.

The awkward thing about a seasonal pattern is that it's almost impossible to see from the inside. In February you're certain you've always felt like this; in July you can't imagine having felt that way at all. It takes something outside your memory to show the shape — which is why a year of ordinary mood tracking is the single most useful thing you can bring to this question.

What the seasonal pattern looks like

Seasonal depression isn't simply feeling flat when it's grey. What distinguishes it is the timing — a consistent onset in autumn or early winter, full remission in spring, repeated across years — and, often, a symptom profile that runs the opposite way to non-seasonal depression.

FeatureWinter-pattern seasonal depressionNon-seasonal depression
SleepSleeping more, hard to wake, still tiredMore often insomnia and early waking
AppetiteIncreased, often craving carbohydrates; weight gainMore often reduced appetite and weight loss
EnergyHeavy, slowed down, everything effortfulVaries; agitation is common
Social lifeWithdrawing, hibernating, declining invitationsWithdrawal too, though less tied to season
TimingStarts autumn, lifts in spring, repeats yearlyOnset any time, often tied to events

Two footnotes to that table. A smaller number of people have the summer pattern instead — low mood arriving with the heat and long days, more often with poor sleep and reduced appetite — and it's frequently missed because it doesn't match the stereotype. And plenty of people have milder seasonal dips, sometimes called the winter blues, which are genuinely unpleasant without meeting the threshold for a diagnosis. The line between them is about impact: whether you're managing but flat, or whether work, relationships and daily functioning are visibly affected.

Where you live matters too. Seasonal patterns are considerably more common at higher latitudes, where winter daylight is short — one reason the same person can move north and meet a version of themselves they've never encountered before.

Why a year of data is what reveals it

Here's the diagnostic problem in one sentence: a clinician needs to see a pattern that repeats over years, and human memory of mood is short, recency-weighted, and heavily colored by how you feel while remembering. Asked in February whether last summer was better, a person in the middle of a seasonal low will genuinely, honestly underrate it.

A daily record has none of those problems. Twelve months of ratings produces a curve, and a seasonal curve is unmistakable when you see it — a long slide from October, a floor somewhere in January or February, a climb through March and April. That's the picture worth bringing to a doctor, because it answers the exact question they need answered.

What makes the year of data even more useful is what sits alongside the ratings. Tag your entries and you can separate season from the things that merely correlate with it:

  • Sleep — timing and duration, which shift with daylight and are often the first thing to move.
  • Time outdoors and daylight exposure — the variable most likely to be doing real work.
  • Exercise, which usually drops in winter for reasons of weather rather than mood.
  • Social contact, which also drops, and independently affects how you feel.
  • Life events — winter carries anniversaries, holidays, financial pressure and family strain, any of which can produce an annual dip that has nothing to do with light.

That last point is the honest caveat: an annual dip is not proof of a light-driven condition. December is hard for a lot of people for reasons that would be hard in July too. Tagged data is what lets you and a clinician tell the difference — and reading your own chart is a skill worth having before you go.

What actually helps

The good news is that seasonal patterns are among the more manageable mood difficulties, partly because they're predictable — you know roughly when it's coming, so you can prepare rather than react. These are the approaches with genuine evidence behind them:

  • Light exposure. The best-supported response. Bright light therapy uses a purpose-built 10,000-lux box, typically for 20–30 minutes soon after waking, positioned to one side rather than stared at. Studies consistently find it effective for winter-pattern symptoms. It isn't right for everyone — certain eye conditions, some medications that increase light sensitivity, and bipolar disorder, where light can trigger a switch into mania — so check with a doctor before starting, and buy a box designed for the purpose rather than a bright lamp.
  • Daylight, actual outdoor. Even a cloudy winter morning is far brighter than indoor lighting. A walk in the first hour or two after waking helps both mood and the body clock, and it's free.
  • A stable sleep schedule. Seasonal disruption tends to push sleep later and longer. Holding a consistent wake time — with light soon after — anchors the whole system, and it is one of the more effective things within your control.
  • Exercise, particularly outdoors and in daylight, which reliably helps mood and is exactly what winter conspires against.
  • Talking therapy. CBT adapted for seasonal patterns has good evidence and, unlike light therapy, tends to keep helping in the winters after treatment ends.
  • Medication. Antidepressants help some people with seasonal depression, sometimes started before the usual onset. That's a conversation with a doctor, not something to arrange yourself.
  • Planning ahead. If your data says the slide starts in mid-October, act in September — light routine in place, social commitments booked, the demanding project scheduled for spring.

Worth mentioning what the evidence doesn't strongly support: vitamin D supplementation has not been shown to treat seasonal depression, though many people at high latitudes are deficient and it's reasonable to ask your doctor about testing for general health reasons. And a fortnight in the sun is lovely and rarely changes the winter you come back to.

When to talk to someone

There's no need to wait until you're certain it's seasonal, or until it's severe. Make an appointment if any of these apply:

  • Low mood is affecting your work, studies, relationships or ability to look after yourself.
  • The pattern has repeated for two or more years and you've been managing it alone.
  • You're sleeping much more, or much less, than usual for weeks at a time.
  • Alcohol or other substances have crept up as a way of getting through it.
  • Your winter low is getting deeper each year.
  • You feel hopeless, or find yourself thinking that people would be better off without you.

That last one deserves its own line. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country now — not after the winter, not once you're sure it's serious enough. Those services exist precisely for the moment when you're not sure.

Bring your record to the appointment if you have one. A year of ratings with a visible seasonal shape answers, in ten seconds, a question that would otherwise take a difficult conversation and a lot of guessing. And if a treatment is started, keep tracking through it — a before-and-after comparison in your own data is more informative than trying to recall in March whether February felt different this time.

Tracking a whole year: paper vs an app

A pocket diary works. One line a day for twelve months is a completely valid way to answer this question, and paper has the advantage of never running out of battery or asking anything of you beyond a pen.

The difficulty is the timescale. Twelve months is a long commitment for a habit with no visible payoff until month nine, and a paper year is a stack of pages nobody plots into a curve. That's where an app earns its place, on the boring parts rather than the interesting ones:

  • Five seconds a day — a five-point mood scale plus tapped factors, which is short enough to survive a whole winter.
  • Reminders and widgets, so a year-long habit doesn't quietly stop in November, which is the month it matters most.
  • Charts over any range, including a full year at a glance and mood broken down by factor — the exact view a seasonal pattern lives in.
  • Apple Health sync for sleep and activity alongside your ratings, since both move with the seasons and both are worth separating from mood itself.
  • Notes and photos for the context a number can't hold, and free-text room for what a dark week was actually like.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON so a year of data becomes one page to hand to your doctor.
  • Private by design: iCloud sync with no account, no email address, on iPhone, iPad and Mac.
  • AI insights over your own history if you want a second look at what moves alongside your mood — descriptive only, never a diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

How is seasonal affective disorder different from the winter blues?

It's a question of degree, and the line is impact rather than mood alone. Winter blues means feeling flatter and slower while still managing your life. A seasonal depression interferes — work, relationships, self-care and daily functioning all take a visible hit, and it repeats in the same months year after year. Only a clinician can make that call.

How long do I need to track before a pattern shows?

A full year gives you the shape; two years make it convincing, because the point of a seasonal diagnosis is that it repeats. You'll often see something suggestive after a single autumn-to-spring stretch — the slide and the recovery are usually the clearest part of the curve.

Does light therapy actually work?

For winter-pattern symptoms, studies consistently find bright light therapy effective, typically 20–30 minutes of 10,000-lux light soon after waking. Talk to a doctor before starting if you have an eye condition, take medication that increases light sensitivity, or have bipolar disorder, where light exposure can trigger a switch into mania.

Can seasonal depression happen in summer?

Yes, though it's less common and often overlooked. The summer pattern tends to bring poor sleep, reduced appetite and agitation rather than the sleeping-and-eating-more picture of winter. If your own chart dips in July rather than January, that's real and worth raising with a doctor rather than dismissing because it doesn't match the stereotype.

Is it just the holidays making me feel bad?

It might be, and that's a genuinely useful thing to separate out. Holiday stress, anniversaries and financial pressure produce annual dips that have nothing to do with daylight. Tagging your entries with what's going on is how you tell them apart — see reading your mood chart for how to compare factors against the trend.

A note on care

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Seasonal affective disorder can only be diagnosed by a qualified clinician, and self-tracking supports professional care rather than replacing it. Speak to a doctor before starting light therapy — particularly if you have an eye condition, take medication that increases light sensitivity, or live with bipolar disorder — and never start or stop a prescribed medication based on your own data. If you feel hopeless, or you are having thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country right away.
