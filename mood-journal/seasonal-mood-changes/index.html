Seasonal Mood Changes

Last updated: August 2026

Everyone feels some difference between a bright June and a dark January; for a smaller group the change is much larger — energy drains away in autumn, everything takes more effort all winter, and spring lifts it, reliably, year after year. When that pattern interferes with daily life, clinicians call it seasonal affective disorder: depression with a seasonal pattern.

A seasonal pattern is nearly impossible to see from inside — in February you're certain you've always felt like this; in July you can't imagine having felt that way. Which is why a year of ordinary mood tracking is the most useful thing you can bring.

What the seasonal pattern looks like

Seasonal depression isn't just feeling flat when it's grey. The signature is timing — onset in autumn, remission in spring, repeated across years — and a profile that often runs opposite to non-seasonal depression.

FeatureWinter-pattern seasonal depressionNon-seasonal depression
SleepSleeping more, hard to wake, still tiredMore often insomnia and early waking
AppetiteIncreased, often craving carbohydrates; weight gainMore often reduced appetite and weight loss
EnergyHeavy, slowed down, everything effortfulVaries; agitation is common
Social lifeWithdrawing, hibernating, declining invitationsWithdrawal too, though less tied to season
TimingStarts autumn, lifts in spring, repeats yearlyOnset any time, often tied to events

A smaller group has the opposite, summer pattern — often missed for breaking the stereotype. Milder dips, the winter blues, are real without meeting the threshold. And seasonal patterns are far more common where winter daylight is short.

Why a year of data is what reveals it

A diagnosis needs a pattern repeating over years, and mood memory is short and recency-weighted — asked in February whether last summer was better, someone mid-low will honestly underrate it.

Twelve months of ratings has no such bias. A seasonal curve is unmistakable: a slide from October, a floor in January or February, a climb through April. Tags separate season from what merely travels with it — sleep, exercise and social contact drop in winter; time outdoors, likely the variable doing real work, drops furthest; and winter carries holidays, anniversaries and financial strain that would be hard in July too. An annual dip isn't proof of a light-driven condition; tagged data tells the difference — see reading your own chart before you go.

What actually helps

Seasonal patterns are unusually manageable because they're predictable: you can prepare rather than react.

  • Light. The best-supported response is a purpose-built 10,000-lux box, 20–30 minutes soon after waking. Not for everyone: some eye conditions, light-sensitizing medications, and bipolar disorder, where light can trigger a switch into mania — check with a doctor first.
  • Daylight, sleep, movement. A cloudy winter morning still far outshines indoor lighting — a walk in the first hour after waking helps mood and body clock. Hold a stable wake time against winter's pull toward later, longer sleep, and keep exercising, ideally outdoors — exactly what winter conspires against.
  • Talking therapy and medication. CBT adapted for seasonal patterns has good evidence and, unlike light therapy, keeps helping in later winters. Antidepressants help some people, sometimes started before the usual onset — a doctor's call.
  • Planning ahead. If your data says the slide starts mid-October, act in September: light routine ready, commitments booked, the hard project scheduled for spring.

Vitamin D hasn't been shown to treat it (testing is still worth asking about), and a fortnight in the sun rarely changes the winter you come back to.

When to talk to someone

Don't wait for certainty. Make an appointment if low mood is affecting work, relationships or self-care; the pattern has repeated two or more years; sleep is far off for weeks; alcohol or other substances have crept up as a way through; each winter is deeper; or you feel hopeless, or catch yourself thinking people would be better off without you.

One deserves its own line: if you are having thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country now — not after the winter, not once you're sure it's serious enough. Those services exist for the moment when you're not sure.

Bring your record — a year of ratings with a visible seasonal shape answers in ten seconds what otherwise takes a difficult conversation. Keep tracking through any treatment: before-and-after data beats recalling in March whether February felt different.

Frequently asked questions

How is seasonal affective disorder different from the winter blues?

A question of degree, and the line is impact. Winter blues means feeling flatter and slower while still managing your life; a seasonal depression interferes — work, relationships, self-care and daily functioning take a visible hit, in the same months year after year. Only a clinician can make that call.

How long do I need to track before a pattern shows?

A full year gives you the shape; two years make it convincing, because the point of a seasonal diagnosis is that it repeats. You'll often see something suggestive after a single autumn-to-spring stretch — the slide and the recovery are usually the clearest part of the curve.

Does light therapy actually work?

For winter-pattern symptoms, studies consistently find bright light therapy effective — typically 20–30 minutes of 10,000-lux light soon after waking. Talk to a doctor first if you have an eye condition, take medication that increases light sensitivity, or have bipolar disorder, where light exposure can trigger a switch into mania.

Can seasonal depression happen in summer?

Yes, though it's less common and often overlooked. The summer pattern tends to bring poor sleep, reduced appetite and agitation rather than winter's sleeping-and-eating-more picture. If your chart dips in July rather than January, that's real — raise it with a doctor rather than dismissing it for breaking the stereotype.

Is it just the holidays making me feel bad?

It might be, and it's genuinely useful to separate out. Holiday stress, anniversaries and financial pressure produce annual dips that have nothing to do with daylight. Tagging your entries with what's going on is how you tell them apart — see reading your mood chart for comparing factors against the trend.

A note on care

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Seasonal affective disorder can only be diagnosed by a qualified clinician, and self-tracking supports professional care rather than replacing it. Speak to a doctor before starting light therapy — particularly if you have an eye condition, take medication that increases light sensitivity, or live with bipolar disorder — and never start or stop a prescribed medication based on your own data. If you feel hopeless, or you are having thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country right away.
