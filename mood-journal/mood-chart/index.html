Reading Your Mood Chart

Last updated: August 2026

A mood chart is your daily ratings plotted against time. The first look is almost always misleading: the eye goes straight to the lowest point, reads "there's the bad day," and stops. Read properly, it answers better questions: where does my mood usually sit, how far does it swing, does it move on a schedule, and what travels with it?

This guide walks the four readings — baseline, range, cycles, factors — with a worked example. Not logging yet? Start with how to track your mood and come back in two weeks.

Before you read: how much data do you need?

A chart of five days is a mood, not a pattern. Roughly:

  • 1–2 weeks: your baseline and how much you vary — nothing about causes.
  • 3–6 weeks: weekly rhythms, and strong single factors — sleep is usually first.
  • 2–3 months: monthly and hormonal cycles, and slow drifts.
  • 6–12 months: seasonal shape, and an honest answer to "am I better than last year?"

Gaps are fine — a blank week is honest. A week reconstructed from memory rewrites the chart toward the story you already believe.

1. Find your baseline

Your baseline is where your mood sits on an ordinary day — the band the line keeps returning to. On a 5-point scale, most people live between 3 and 4.

It's the most useful number here: a 3 means something different for someone who lives at 4 than at 2, and it moves slowly, so a drift means more than one bad Tuesday. A baseline sliding from 4 to 3 over two months outweighs any single low point — the change that's invisible from the inside.

Cover the extremes with your thumb, see where the bulk of the line sits, and compare this month to last.

2. Judge the range

Range is how far your mood travels around that baseline. Two people share a 3.5 baseline: one moves between 3 and 4, the other bounces between 1 and 5. Three questions:

  • How wide is a typical week? One point is ordinary; three or four, repeatedly, is worth attention.
  • How fast are the transitions? A slow two-week slope and a same-day 5-to-1 drop are different phenomena.
  • How long does a low last? A day-long dip differs from two weeks at the bottom.

If the swings are the main story, the mood swings guide covers drivers and when to see a professional.

3. Look for cycles

Cycles turn "randomly bad sometimes" into something you can plan around. Four to check:

  • Daily. Needs more than one log a day — heavier mornings, or a late-evening dip.
  • Weekly. The most common finding: Sunday evenings, a Wednesday trough, a genuine Friday lift.
  • Monthly. Hormonal cycles are among the clearest patterns in mood data; give them two or three months.
  • Seasonal. Slow, and easy to blame on whatever else was going on — see seasonal mood changes.

Cycles appear in any long record, real or not — trust one that repeats three times with a plausible mechanism.

4. Factors: a worked example

Here the chart stops describing and starts explaining — and fooling yourself gets easy. Two weeks of entries:

DayMood (1–5)SleepNote
Mon36hBusy start
Tue25hLate night before
Wed25h30Flat, no reason
Thu48hSlept in
Fri47h30Walk, saw friends
Sat59h—
Sun38hDreading the week
Mon26hDeadline
Tue25hDeadline, no walk
Wed37hDeadline done
Thu48hGym
Fri47h—
Sat48h30Quiet day
Sun38hSunday feeling again

What this supports: a baseline around 3, a range of 2 to 5, a repeated Sunday dip, and a strong link between nights under six hours and mood at 2.

What it doesn't prove: that short sleep caused the low mood. Both weeks had deadlines, which shorten sleep and flatten mood independently; low mood also disrupts sleep, so the arrow may run backwards. And the Sunday dip isn't sleep — Sunday sleep was fine both weeks.

To trust a correlation: count the exceptions (a factor that holds half the time is a coincidence with good PR), check the lag (sleep and alcohol land the next day), look for a third thing driving both — deadlines, illness, travel — and test one change at a time.

Paper chart vs app: an honest comparison

Graph paper works, and a spreadsheet can sort by weekday. What both cost is follow-through — nobody re-sorts a spreadsheet by factor every month. The app side of the trade:

  • Charts drawn as you log, over any period — the trend is always current.
  • Mood broken down by factor — the comparisons above, without arithmetic.
  • Every point attached to its note or photo — a dip is one tap from its reason.
  • Apple Health sync — sleep arrives on its own.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON — for appointments or your own analysis.
  • AI insights over your own history, free with optional premium — a second read, not advice.

Whatever draws the chart, the interpretation stays yours — this is a self-tracking tool, not a diagnostic one. If something worries you, bring the chart to a doctor or therapist — far more concrete than memory.

Frequently asked questions

How many weeks of data do I need before the chart is useful?

Three to four weeks for weekly rhythms and strong single factors like sleep; two to three months for monthly cycles; most of a year for seasonal shape. One or two weeks shows your baseline and how much you vary — already worth having.

My chart looks completely random. Am I doing it wrong?

Probably not. Some charts are genuinely noisy, and a flat scatter still says something real: no single factor is dominating your mood. Sort entries by weekday, and check next-day effects rather than same-day ones — lagged patterns hide in plain sight. Still random after two months? That's an answer too.

Does a low point on my chart mean something is wrong?

No. Everyone's chart has low points, and a bad day is not a diagnosis. What deserves attention is duration and direction: a low persisting most of the day for more than two weeks, or a baseline drifting downward for months. Those are worth discussing with a doctor or therapist — bring the chart.

Should I average my mood ratings?

A monthly average is a fair summary of the baseline, but averaging throws away the range — often the more interesting half of the story. Look at the average and the spread together, as described in the mood swings guide.

Can I show my mood chart to a therapist?

Yes, and most will welcome it. A dated chart with factor tags turns a vague summary into something specific to work from. Export a PDF for a clean version, or CSV/XLSX for the underlying numbers.

A note on wellbeing

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. A mood chart is a self-tracking tool, not a diagnostic instrument — patterns in it are a good starting point for a conversation with a doctor or therapist, never a substitute for one. If low mood persists most of the day for more than two weeks, or you notice lasting changes in sleep, appetite, or your ability to manage day to day, please speak to a healthcare professional. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline in your country right away — support is available now.
