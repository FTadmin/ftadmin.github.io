Mood Journal Prompts

Last updated: August 2026

The hardest part of keeping a mood journal isn't finding the time. It's the blank field — the moment when you've rated the day, you know there's more to say, and nothing comes. A prompt solves that in about a second, because answering a specific question is far easier than producing a thought from nothing.

Below are 44 prompts, grouped by the kind of day you're having. They're meant to be used one at a time and answered in a sentence or two, not worked through in order. If you're just getting started, the guide on how to track your mood covers the rating and the factor tags that these notes sit alongside.

How to use these prompts

Four rules, and they matter more than which prompt you pick.

  • One prompt, one entry. Not five. A journal that takes twenty minutes gets abandoned in a fortnight, and it gets abandoned first on the days you most needed it.
  • Two sentences is a complete answer. There's no length you're failing to reach. "Tired. The meeting went better than I expected" is a real entry.
  • Answer honestly or skip it. A polished answer is worse than no answer, because it becomes part of a record you'll later read as true.
  • Don't rotate for the sake of variety. If one question keeps producing something useful, keep asking it. Repetition is what makes answers comparable across weeks.

Daily check-in prompts

For ordinary days. Pick one or two and keep them stable for a few weeks — the value is in comparing this Tuesday's answer to last Tuesday's, which only works if the question stayed the same.

Morning

  1. How did I sleep, and how am I arriving into today?
  2. What's the one thing that would make today feel okay, even if nothing else goes right?
  3. What am I quietly dreading, and how big is it really?
  4. What does my body feel like right now — before I've thought about anything?
  5. What do I need more of today: rest, movement, company, or quiet?
  6. If today goes the way I expect, how will I feel at 9pm?

Evening

  1. What was the strongest feeling I had today, and when did it show up?
  2. What's one thing that went better than I expected?
  3. When did I feel most like myself today?
  4. What took the most out of me? What gave something back?
  5. Is there anything I'm carrying into tomorrow that I could put down tonight?
  6. In one word: how was today? Then in one more sentence: why that word?

If the feeling is there but the word won't come, the emotion wheel is the fastest way to move from "bad" to something specific enough to be useful.

Prompts for hard days

These are written to describe rather than to fix. On a hard day, the goal of a journal entry is to get the weight out of your head and onto a page — not to solve anything, not to be fair, and not to arrive anywhere in particular. Answer in whatever tone shows up.

  1. What happened, in plain factual terms, with no interpretation attached?
  2. What am I feeling right now — and is there a second feeling underneath the first one?
  3. Where do I feel this in my body?
  4. What's the story I'm telling myself about this? What else could be true?
  5. Is this a today problem, a this-week problem, or a this-year problem?
  6. What part of this is actually mine to carry, and what belongs to someone else?
  7. Have I eaten, slept, moved, or been outside today? (Genuinely — check.)
  8. What would I need to hear right now if a friend were saying it to me?
  9. What's the smallest thing that would make the next hour 10% easier?
  10. What do I want to remember about today when I read this back in a month?

If you're in the middle of something intense rather than looking back on it, writing may be too much to ask of yourself, and that's fine. A short grounding exercise first, then a one-line entry afterwards, is a perfectly good version of this. The structured skills in the DBT guide are built for exactly these hours.

One boundary worth keeping: journaling helps when it's describing, and stops helping when it turns into circling the same painful thought for an hour. If an entry starts to feel like rumination, put the pen down — that's the signal to stop, not to try harder.

Prompts for good days

The most-skipped category, and the most quietly valuable. Nobody feels an urge to journal when things are going well — which is exactly why most people's records read as an unbroken run of difficulty. A log made only of hard days is both inaccurate and discouraging to read back, and it hides the one thing you actually want: evidence of what helps.

  1. What made today good? Be specific — "the weather" and "I had lunch outside with someone I like" are not the same information.
  2. What did I do differently today, if anything?
  3. What conditions made this possible — sleep, a lighter schedule, being outdoors, seeing someone, saying no to something?
  4. What did I enjoy that I hadn't planned to?
  5. Who was I with, and what was different about that?
  6. What would I like more of, and what would it take to arrange it?
  7. What am I grateful for today that isn't the obvious answer?
  8. What do I want to remember about how this felt, for a day when I've forgotten it's possible?

That last one earns its place. On a low day it's genuinely hard to believe good ones happen, and a note in your own handwriting from three weeks ago is more convincing than anything anyone can tell you.

Prompts for patterns and for self-compassion

Finding patterns

These are for the monthly review — the five minutes with your history that turns a pile of entries into something you can use. Read a few weeks back, then answer:

  1. What shows up in my entries more often than I would have guessed?
  2. What do my worst days have in common? What about my best ones?
  3. Which day of the week is consistently hardest, and does the reason still hold?
  4. What was I worried about a month ago, and how did it actually turn out?
  5. What have I been avoiding for long enough that avoiding it is now the problem?
  6. What did I think was a mood problem that looks more like a sleep, workload or hunger problem in writing?
  7. What advice have I given myself repeatedly and not taken?

Answers here pair naturally with the numbers. The mood chart guide covers baseline, range and how to tell a genuine factor from a coincidence — the notes tell you what kind of day it was, and the chart tells you how often.

Self-compassion

For days when the tone in your head has turned sharp. Warmth toward yourself isn't self-indulgence; it's the condition under which people are able to look honestly at what happened.

  1. What would I say to a friend in exactly this situation?
  2. What am I blaming myself for that was mostly circumstance?
  3. What did I do today that took effort, even if it doesn't look like much from outside?
  4. What was I dealing with at the same time that I'm giving myself no credit for?
  5. What standard am I holding myself to, and would I apply it to anyone else?
  6. What's one kind, true sentence I can say about myself right now? (True matters more than kind.)
  7. If today was all I had in me, what would be enough?

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

A paper journal is a genuinely good place for these prompts. Handwriting is slower than typing, and the slowness is a feature when you're reflecting — you think differently at the speed of a pen, and there's nothing on the page competing for your attention.

What paper does badly is everything after the writing. It can't be searched, so "when did I last feel like this?" means flipping pages. It can't put your words next to a rating and a sleep tag. And it isn't with you at 11pm on the evening you most want to write something down. The split, honestly:

  • The rating takes about five seconds — a tap on the 5-point scale plus a factor or two — so the prompt is the only part that needs thought.
  • Free-text notes and photos sit alongside the number, which means the answer to tonight's prompt stays attached to the day it describes.
  • Charts and trends put your written answers next to how the days actually scored, which is what makes the monthly pattern prompts answerable rather than speculative.
  • Reminders and widgets so the check-in happens on ordinary days, not just memorable ones — ordinary days are most of the data.
  • iCloud sync with no account and no email address, on iPhone, iPad and Mac. This is private writing; it stays on your devices and nobody asks you to register.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON when you want to bring specifics to a therapist rather than a summary reconstructed in the waiting room.
  • AI insights over your own history, free with an optional premium tier, offering a second read on recurring themes across your entries. Useful as a mirror; it isn't advice and it isn't a clinician.

Use both if it suits you — many people write long-form on paper and keep the daily rating on a phone. Whichever you choose, a journal is a self-monitoring tool: it supports DBT-style self-observation and makes conversations with a professional far more concrete, but it doesn't treat or diagnose anything. Two honest sentences a day beats a beautiful notebook you stopped opening in March.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a mood journal entry be?

One or two sentences. Length is not the point, and long entries are the main reason people quit — they get skipped first on busy days and hard days, which are the days worth recording. Consistency beats depth every time; see how to track your mood.

Should I use the same prompt every day?

Repetition is underrated. Asking the same question daily makes answers comparable across weeks, which is where patterns live. A reasonable setup is one fixed daily question plus a situational one — hard day, good day, or self-compassion — when the day calls for it.

What if I have nothing to write?

Then write that. "Nothing much happened, felt steady" is a genuine data point, and ordinary days are most of your record. If the blank is really about not knowing what you feel rather than nothing having happened, start from the body — tight, heavy, restless — or use the emotion wheel.

Is journaling ever unhelpful?

It can be, when it turns from describing into circling the same painful thought. Journaling helps when it gets something out of your head; it stops helping when it keeps you inside it. If an entry starts to feel like rumination, stop and try a grounding exercise instead — that's the right response, not writing harder.

Should I write in the morning or the evening?

Evening suits reflection and works for most people, because the day is complete. Morning suits intention-setting and catches mood before the day shapes it. Doing both is the fullest picture, but pick one to start with — the one you'll actually keep.

A note on wellbeing

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Journaling is a self-reflection and self-tracking practice, not therapy and not a diagnostic instrument — it can make a conversation with a doctor or therapist far more concrete, but it never replaces one. If low mood persists most of the day for more than two weeks, if writing consistently leaves you feeling worse, or if emotions are disrupting your daily life, please talk to a healthcare professional. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline in your country right away — support is available now.
