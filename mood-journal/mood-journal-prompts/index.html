Mood Journal Prompts

Last updated: August 2026

The hardest part of a mood journal is the blank field — day rated, more to say, nothing comes. A specific question fixes that.

Below: 44 prompts by kind of day, one at a time, a sentence or two. New? Start with how to track your mood.

How to use these prompts

One prompt per entry. Two sentences is a complete answer. Answer honestly or skip — polish pollutes the record. Repeat what works; repetition makes answers comparable. Which group:

Daily check-in prompts

Pick one or two and keep them stable, so this Tuesday compares to last.

Morning

  1. How did I sleep, and how am I arriving into today?
  2. What's the one thing that would make today feel okay, even if nothing else goes right?
  3. What am I quietly dreading, and how big is it really?
  4. What does my body feel like right now — before I've thought about anything?
  5. What do I need more of today: rest, movement, company, or quiet?
  6. If today goes the way I expect, how will I feel at 9pm?

Evening

  1. What was the strongest feeling I had today, and when did it show up?
  2. What's one thing that went better than I expected?
  3. When did I feel most like myself today?
  4. What took the most out of me? What gave something back?
  5. Is there anything I'm carrying into tomorrow that I could put down tonight?
  6. In one word: how was today? Then in one more sentence: why that word?

The emotion wheel turns "bad" into something specific.

Prompts for hard days

These describe, not fix: get the weight out of your head, any tone.

  1. What happened, in plain factual terms, with no interpretation attached?
  2. What am I feeling right now — and is there a second feeling underneath the first one?
  3. Where do I feel this in my body?
  4. What's the story I'm telling myself about this? What else could be true?
  5. Is this a today problem, a this-week problem, or a this-year problem?
  6. What part of this is actually mine to carry, and what belongs to someone else?
  7. Have I eaten, slept, moved, or been outside today? (Genuinely — check.)
  8. What would I need to hear right now if a friend were saying it to me?
  9. What's the smallest thing that would make the next hour 10% easier?
  10. What do I want to remember about today when I read this back in a month?

Mid-crisis: a grounding exercise, then one line. The DBT guide covers these hours. Writing becoming circling? That's the signal to stop.

Prompts for good days

Most skipped, most valuable: a record of only hard days hides what helps.

  1. What made today good? Be specific — "the weather" and "I had lunch outside with someone I like" are not the same information.
  2. What did I do differently today, if anything?
  3. What conditions made this possible — sleep, a lighter schedule, being outdoors, seeing someone, saying no to something?
  4. What did I enjoy that I hadn't planned to?
  5. Who was I with, and what was different about that?
  6. What would I like more of, and what would it take to arrange it?
  7. What am I grateful for today that isn't the obvious answer?
  8. What do I want to remember about how this felt, for a day when I've forgotten it's possible?

The last prompt is proof, in your own writing, that good days happen.

Prompts for patterns and for self-compassion

Finding patterns

For the monthly review: read a few weeks back, then answer.

  1. What shows up in my entries more often than I would have guessed?
  2. What do my worst days have in common? What about my best ones?
  3. Which day of the week is consistently hardest, and does the reason still hold?
  4. What was I worried about a month ago, and how did it actually turn out?
  5. What have I been avoiding for long enough that avoiding it is now the problem?
  6. What did I think was a mood problem that looks more like a sleep, workload or hunger problem in writing?
  7. What advice have I given myself repeatedly and not taken?

The mood chart guide adds the numbers.

Self-compassion

For when your inner tone turns sharp.

  1. What would I say to a friend in exactly this situation?
  2. What am I blaming myself for that was mostly circumstance?
  3. What did I do today that took effort, even if it doesn't look like much from outside?
  4. What was I dealing with at the same time that I'm giving myself no credit for?
  5. What standard am I holding myself to, and would I apply it to anyone else?
  6. What's one kind, true sentence I can say about myself right now? (True matters more than kind.)
  7. If today was all I had in me, what would be enough?

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

Paper suits these prompts; handwriting slows you down, usefully. But it can't search "when did I last feel like this?" and isn't there at 11pm. The app's half:

  • A five-second rating, with notes and photos attached to their day.
  • Reminders, iCloud sync without an account, export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON.
  • AI insights over your history, free with optional premium — a mirror, not advice.

A journal is self-monitoring, not therapy; two honest sentences beat the notebook abandoned in March.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a mood journal entry be?

One or two sentences. Long entries are the main reason people quit — they get skipped on busy days and hard days, the ones worth recording. Consistency beats depth every time; see how to track your mood.

Should I use the same prompt every day?

Repetition is underrated: the same question daily makes answers comparable across weeks, which is where patterns live. A good setup is one fixed daily question plus a situational one — hard day, good day, or self-compassion — when the day calls for it.

What if I have nothing to write?

Write that. "Nothing much happened, felt steady" is a real data point, and ordinary days are most of your record. If the blank is about not knowing what you feel, start from the body — tight, heavy, restless — or use the emotion wheel.

Is journaling ever unhelpful?

It can be, when describing turns into circling the same painful thought. Journaling helps when it gets something out of your head; it stops helping when it keeps you inside it. If an entry starts to feel like rumination, stop and try a grounding exercise instead.

Should I write in the morning or the evening?

Evening suits reflection and works for most people — the day is complete. Morning suits intention-setting and catches mood before the day shapes it. Both is the fullest picture, but start with the one you'll actually keep.

A note on wellbeing

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Journaling is a self-reflection and self-tracking practice, not therapy and not a diagnostic instrument — it can make a conversation with a doctor or therapist far more concrete, but it never replaces one. If low mood persists most of the day for more than two weeks, if writing consistently leaves you feeling worse, or if emotions are disrupting your daily life, please talk to a healthcare professional. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline in your country right away — support is available now.
