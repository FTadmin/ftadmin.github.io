BPD and Mood Tracking

Last updated: August 2026

If you're reading about borderline personality disorder, there's a good chance you recognized something — in yourself, or in someone you love — and want it explained without jargon and without alarm. That's what this page is for. It walks through the nine criteria clinicians use, in ordinary language, and then covers something practical: why keeping a daily record of your moods is unusually useful when your emotions move fast.

One thing first, and it matters more than anything else here. Only a qualified clinician can diagnose BPD, through a proper assessment over time. A list of criteria on a web page is a vocabulary, not a verdict — most people recognize themselves in several of these, because they describe ordinary human experiences turned up loud. If this feels close to home, the useful next step is a conversation with a doctor or mental health professional, not a conclusion drawn tonight.

What BPD is, in plain terms

Borderline personality disorder is, at its core, a difficulty with emotional regulation. Feelings arrive faster, hit harder, and take longer to settle than they do for most people — the same event that gives someone else a bad afternoon can give you a bad week, and it's not a matter of trying harder. Much of what looks from the outside like drama or instability is what someone does to cope with an internal weather system that is genuinely more extreme.

The name is a historical accident, from an old idea that these difficulties sat on the "border" between two other categories. It describes nothing useful, many clinicians dislike it, and some countries prefer the term emotionally unstable personality disorder. Neither name captures the part worth knowing: BPD responds well to treatment. Structured therapies — DBT most famously, along with mentalization-based therapy and schema therapy — have a strong track record, and a large proportion of people no longer meet the criteria after a few years. Whatever you may have read, this is not a life sentence.

The nine criteria, in plain language

Clinicians work from a list of nine features, and a diagnosis generally requires five or more that have been present since early adulthood, across different situations, and that cause real difficulty in daily life. Here they are, translated out of manual-speak:

CriterionWhat it can look like day to day
Fear of abandonmentAn unbearable reaction to being left, even briefly or unintentionally — an unanswered message reading as proof you're about to lose someone
Unstable relationshipsCloseness that swings between idealizing someone and feeling deeply let down by them, sometimes within the same week
Unstable sense of selfYour goals, values, tastes, even your sense of who you are shifting depending on who you're with
ImpulsivitySpending, driving, substances, food, or sex done in ways that bring relief in the moment and regret afterwards
Self-harm or suicidal behaviorRecurring urges, threats or acts of self-injury, often as a way to manage unbearable feeling rather than to end life
Emotional instabilityIntense mood shifts that last hours rather than weeks — irritable, anxious, despairing and back again inside a day
Chronic emptinessA persistent hollow feeling that isn't quite sadness and doesn't fill up, however busy or loved you are
Intense angerAnger out of proportion to the trigger, hard to bring down, often followed by shame
Stress-related dissociation or paranoiaUnder pressure, feeling unreal or detached from yourself, or briefly convinced everyone has turned against you

Read gently. Everyone has bad days that touch several of these, and recognizing three or four of them does not mean you have a personality disorder — it may mean you're exhausted, grieving, in an unhappy relationship, or living with something else entirely. The threshold clinicians care about is a pattern that is long-standing, wide-ranging, and genuinely disruptive.

Who can actually diagnose this

A psychiatrist, clinical psychologist, or similarly qualified mental health professional — through interviews over time, a history that goes back to your teens or early twenties, and a careful look at what else might explain the same picture. No questionnaire, no online quiz, and no article can do it, including this one.

The reason isn't gatekeeping. It's that several very different things produce overlapping symptoms, and the treatment differs sharply:

  • Bipolar disorder also involves mood instability, but in episodes lasting days to weeks, often with changed sleep need and energy — not shifts that resolve within an afternoon.
  • Complex PTSD from prolonged trauma shares emotional dysregulation, unstable self-image and relationship difficulties, and the distinction takes real clinical skill.
  • ADHD brings its own impulsivity and emotional intensity, and is frequently missed in adults, especially women.
  • Depression, anxiety, and substance use can each produce a snapshot that looks like several criteria at once.

Self-diagnosis has an obvious appeal — a name for a thing that has never had one, and a community who describe your inner life accurately for the first time. That relief is real and worth honoring. It's also worth taking to a clinician, because if the underlying picture is ADHD or complex trauma, the wrong frame can send you down a path of treatment that doesn't fit.

If you've recently been given the diagnosis instead: it's information about patterns, not a statement about your character. People do get better, and many find the label most useful for the door it opens to the right kind of therapy.

Why tracking helps with rapid shifts

Here's the clinical detail that makes daily tracking genuinely useful rather than just tidy: BPD mood shifts happen on a timescale that ordinary recall can't hold. Where a depressive episode is measured in weeks and shows up plainly in a summary of the month, a BPD mood shift can be an hour of despair between two reasonably okay hours — and by Friday, nobody remembers Tuesday afternoon accurately.

That has three practical consequences.

It helps clinicians tell things apart. The difference between BPD and bipolar disorder is largely a question of duration and trigger, and a record showing shifts that resolve inside a day — usually after something interpersonal — is far better evidence than trying to describe it from memory in a twenty-minute appointment.

It finds your triggers. Tag what's around each entry — a conflict, a missed reply, poor sleep, alcohol, a busy week — and after a month the repeated pairings surface. Most people find one or two they never suspected, and knowing them turns a shift from something that happens to you into something with an entry point. Our guide to mood swings goes deeper into pattern-finding.

It shows progress that feeling can't. The cruelest thing about a hard week in recovery is that it erases the memory of the good ones — six calm weeks vanish behind three bad days. A chart doesn't have that bias. Being able to see that this month has fewer extreme days than March did is, for a lot of people, the single most encouraging thing tracking gives them.

One caution worth stating plainly: tracking can tip into checking. If rating your mood starts to feel like an anxious hourly audit of yourself, scale back to once a day, or take a break, and mention it to your therapist. The record is meant to serve you, not supervise you.

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

A notebook works. Plenty of people have tracked their moods for years with a pen and a ruled page, and paper has real advantages: no screen, no notifications, no temptation to check it fifteen times a day, and complete privacy from anything networked.

What paper struggles with is the exact thing that matters here — seeing shape across time, and being available at the moment a mood turns. If you want the pattern rather than the diary, an app does the boring parts better:

  • Five seconds to log: a five-point scale plus tapped factors, quick enough to catch a shift while it's happening rather than reconstructing it later.
  • Charts and trends that show mood over time and broken down by factor — the view that reveals rapid within-day patterns a monthly summary hides.
  • Notes and photos when a rating isn't enough, and reminders so the habit survives a hard week.
  • PDF, XLSX, CSV and JSON export to bring to an appointment, so your clinician sees three months instead of your recollection of three days.
  • Genuine privacy: iCloud sync with no account and no email address, plus Apple Health sync if you want sleep and activity alongside your ratings.
  • AI insights over your own history if you want a second look at your patterns — descriptive, optional, and never a diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

Can I diagnose BPD myself from the nine criteria?

No — and this is the one point worth being firm about. Diagnosis needs a qualified clinician, a history going back years, and a careful look at what else could explain the same symptoms, including bipolar disorder, complex PTSD and ADHD. Use the criteria as language for describing your experience to a professional, not as a self-assessment.

How are BPD mood swings different from bipolar disorder?

Mostly duration and trigger. BPD shifts tend to be rapid — hours rather than weeks — and usually follow something interpersonal, then settle. Bipolar episodes last days to weeks, come with changed sleep need and energy, and can appear without an obvious trigger. This is exactly the distinction a daily record makes visible; see mood swings: causes and patterns.

Does BPD get better?

Yes, and this deserves saying loudly because the internet often suggests otherwise. Studies consistently find that with structured therapy — and often with time alone — a large proportion of people no longer meet the criteria years later. DBT skills are the best-known approach, but they aren't the only effective one.

What should I actually track?

Keep it small enough to survive a bad day: one daily rating, the factors around it (sleep, conflict, alcohol, workload), and a line of notes when something stands out. If you're working with a therapist, ask what would be most useful to them — many will suggest something close to a DBT diary card.

I'm supporting someone with BPD. What helps?

Validation more than solutions — saying that a reaction makes sense given how it feels is not the same as agreeing it's proportionate, and it defuses far more than reasoning does. Be consistent about what you can and can't offer, keep your own support in place, and if there's immediate risk of harm, contact local emergency services.

A note on care

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Borderline personality disorder can only be diagnosed by a qualified mental health professional after a proper assessment — nothing here can confirm or rule it out, and self-tracking supports care rather than replacing it. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, or you are worried about someone who is, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country right away. Support exists, and reaching for it is not an overreaction.
