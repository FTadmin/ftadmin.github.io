Grounding Techniques

Last updated: August 2026

Grounding is what you do when a feeling has gotten bigger than your ability to think about it — a panic surge, a wave of dread, a flashback, or the strange flat distance of dissociation. The goal is modest and specific: get your attention back into the present moment and your body out of full alarm, so that the feeling becomes something you can ride out rather than something that carries you off.

What follows are techniques that hold up in real moments, sorted by how much distress you're in, because that turns out to be the thing that decides which ones work. A gentle noticing exercise is excellent at a four out of ten and completely useless at a nine — not because you're doing it wrong, but because at a nine your thinking brain isn't the part that's available. Pick from the right shelf and grounding stops feeling like advice that doesn't apply to you.

What grounding is — and what it isn't

Grounding is a distress tolerance skill: it gets you through the next fifteen minutes without making anything worse. It is not designed to solve the problem, process the memory, or address why the feeling turned up. Expecting it to do that is the fastest route to deciding grounding doesn't work for you.

Two clarifications worth keeping in mind. First, grounding is not the same as distraction, though they overlap — distraction takes your attention away, while grounding puts it somewhere specific and physical, usually something you can see, touch or feel right now. Second, it isn't relaxation. You may still feel awful afterwards; the win is that you feel awful and present, which is the state in which you can decide what to do next.

It also works better when it's rehearsed. Practicing a technique on a calm Tuesday feels pointless, and it's exactly what makes the technique available at a bad Saturday's nine out of ten, when nothing new is going to be learned. Treat the calm-day practice as the actual training.

Techniques by intensity

Match the technique to the moment. As distress rises, the useful techniques get more physical and less cognitive — and above about an eight, the ones that work are the ones that change your body chemistry directly rather than asking anything of your attention.

Mild (1–3)Uneasy, tense, mind drifting to worries5-4-3-2-1 senses; naming what you see out loud; a warm drink held in both hands; putting your bare feet on the floor and noticing it
Moderate (4–6)Clearly anxious, racing thoughts, restlessnessPaced breathing with a longer out-breath; a walk with attention on your footfall; categories game (name ten cities); holding an ice cube; stretching the shoulders and jaw
High (7–8)Trembling, chest tight, thinking has narrowedCold water on the face or wrists; bilateral movement — walking, alternating taps left and right; strong sour or minty taste; pushing hard against a wall for ten seconds
Crisis (9–10)Panic, dissociation, urges you don't want to act onCold pack over the eyes and cheeks; a short burst of intense exercise; slow paced breathing; then contact a person — a friend, a helpline, your therapist's out-of-hours number

The higher rows come straight from the DBT distress tolerance module, where they're taught as TIPP. One health note before you use them: the cold-water and intense-exercise skills deliberately change your heart rate, so check with your doctor first if you have a heart condition, an eating disorder, are pregnant, or take medication affecting your heart rate. The breathing and sensory techniques are gentle and suit almost everyone.

The four core techniques, properly

5-4-3-2-1

The best-known one, and usually taught too fast. Slowly name five things you can see, four you can feel, three you can hear, two you can smell, one you can taste. The value is in going slowly and being specific — not "a chair" but "the scratch on the arm of the blue chair." Say them out loud if you can; speaking recruits more of your attention than thinking does. If you lose count, start again; the counting isn't the point.

Temperature

The fastest-acting technique on the list. Cold water splashed on the face, or a cold pack held over your eyes and upper cheeks while you breathe out slowly, triggers the body's dive reflex, which slows the heart rate within seconds. A shorter version for public places: hold your wrists under a cold tap, or hold an ice cube until it genuinely hurts a little. The physical shock gives your nervous system something undeniable and immediate to respond to.

Paced breathing

The mechanism is specific: making your out-breath longer than your in-breath activates the calming branch of the nervous system. Breathe in for a count of four, out for six — or in for four, hold four, out for six. Keep it comfortable; forcing very deep breaths can make you lightheaded and feel worse. Five or six of these is usually enough to notice a change, and unlike anything else on this list you can do it in a meeting with nobody knowing.

Bilateral movement

Anything that alternates left and right rhythmically: walking, especially outside; slow alternating taps on your knees or crossed arms; passing an object hand to hand. Rhythmic, alternating movement seems to help settle a fear response, and it's the reason a walk so often works better than an hour of trying to think your way out. A short walk with your attention on your feet is two techniques at once.

What to do afterwards

The part nobody covers. When grounding works, you're not fine — you're present, shaky, and often quite tired. Coming down from a big alarm response uses real physical resources, and the hour afterwards deserves treating as recovery rather than as a return to normal service.

  1. Warmth and something to eat or drink. Blood sugar and body temperature both take a hit. This is not a small thing; it's often what stops a second wave.
  2. Lower the demands for the next hour if you possibly can. Cancel the optional item. A second surge is much more likely when you go straight back into what triggered the first.
  3. Tell someone, if you have someone. Not necessarily to analyze it — being with another person is itself regulating.
  4. Write down three things while it's fresh: how high it got, what preceded it, and which technique you used and whether it helped.
  5. Skip the post-mortem for now. Working out what it all means is a job for a calm day, ideally with your therapist. Straight afterwards, analysis usually turns into rumination.

That fourth point is worth dwelling on, because it compounds. Every entry adds to a personal answer to the question "what actually works for me?" — and the honest answer is almost never the generic list. Some people find 5-4-3-2-1 useless and cold water reliable. Others find the opposite. A few weeks of noting which technique you reached for, and whether it helped, produces a short ranked list that's worth more than any article, this one included. Recording the trigger alongside it feeds directly into tracking anxiety and finding what sets these moments off in the first place.

Keeping a list: paper vs an app

You need two things: your techniques written somewhere you'll find them in a bad moment, and a record of which ones worked. An index card in your wallet does the first job beautifully — it needs no battery, no unlocking, and no scrolling past notifications, and if that's what you'll use, use it.

The second job is where paper struggles. Notes on loose cards don't accumulate into an answer, and nobody counts up two months of them. That's the boring part an app handles:

  • Five seconds to log a moment — a simple scale plus tapped factors — which is about all anyone can manage while still shaky.
  • Free-text notes and photos for which technique you used and whether it helped, kept with the entry instead of on a card that gets lost.
  • Charts and trends that show how often the spikes happen and which factors keep showing up alongside them.
  • Widgets and reminders, so logging takes one tap from the home screen and the calm days get recorded too.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON — useful when a therapist asks how often this has been happening and you'd rather show than guess.
  • Private by default: iCloud sync with no account and no email address, on iPhone, iPad and Mac.

Frequently asked questions

Why doesn't 5-4-3-2-1 work for me?

Most often because it's being used at the wrong intensity. Sensory noticing needs some working attention, and above roughly a seven out of ten that attention isn't available. Try a physical technique instead — cold water, paced breathing, movement — and save 5-4-3-2-1 for earlier in the climb, when you first notice things tightening.

How long should grounding take to work?

Temperature and paced breathing often shift something within a minute or two. Sensory and movement techniques take five to ten minutes. If nothing has moved after about fifteen minutes, switch technique rather than trying harder at the same one — and if you're in real crisis, reach out to a person or a crisis line rather than continuing alone.

Can grounding help with dissociation?

It's one of the main uses. When you feel unreal or detached, the physical techniques tend to work best: cold, strong tastes, texture under your fingers, feet firmly on the floor, naming objects out loud. If dissociation is frequent, it's worth raising with a professional — it often has a history behind it that deserves proper support.

Should I practice when I'm calm?

Yes, and it's the single biggest thing that makes grounding work when it counts. A technique you've run through a few times on an ordinary day is available automatically; one you've only read about has to be recalled and learned in the worst possible conditions. Two minutes a few times a week is plenty.

Is grounding a substitute for therapy?

No. Grounding gets you through the moment; it doesn't address what's generating the moments. It works best alongside proper support — see DBT skills for the wider framework these techniques come from, and speak to a doctor or therapist if you're needing them often.

A note on care

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. Grounding techniques help you get through difficult moments; they do not treat an underlying condition, and needing them often is a good reason to speak with a doctor or therapist. Check with your doctor before using the cold-water or intense-exercise techniques if you have a heart condition, an eating disorder, are pregnant, or take medication that affects your heart rate. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or of suicide, please contact your local emergency services or a crisis line in your country right away — that is what they are there for.
