How to Track Your Mood

Last updated: August 2026

Mood tracking is the small habit of noting how you feel on a simple scale, at roughly the same time each day, along with a few things that might be shaping it. A single entry takes seconds and tells you almost nothing. The value arrives weeks later, when a run of small entries shows you something you could never have held in your head — that your flattest days follow short nights, that Sunday evenings have a shape of their own, or that the low mood you assumed was constant actually lifts by midweek.

This guide covers what to log, how often to log it, which contributing factors are worth tagging, and why a plain record you keep beats an elaborate one you abandon. Once you have a few weeks of entries, the companion guide on reading your mood chart picks up where this one ends.

What to log in a single entry

A useful entry has four parts, and only the first is essential:

  • A rating. A 5-point scale is the sweet spot — enough resolution to see movement, few enough options that you never agonize over the choice. Very low, low, okay, good, very good. Pick the number that matches how the last few hours actually felt, not how you think the day should have gone.
  • The time. Mood has a daily rhythm, and a 3 logged at 7am means something different from a 3 logged at 11pm. If your app stamps the time automatically, you get this for free.
  • A factor or two. Sleep, work, exercise, a hard conversation. Tags are what turn a line on a chart into an explanation — see the table below.
  • One line of text. Not an essay. "Slept badly, meeting ran long" is enough to bring the day back six weeks from now. A photo works the same way, and often faster.

What you should not do is rate the day you wish you had. A record that quietly rounds bad days upward is worse than no record, because you will eventually make decisions from it. Nobody else has to see this.

If putting a name to the feeling is the hard part, that is normal and it is a skill you can build. The emotion wheel gives you a vocabulary to move from "bad" to something specific enough to act on.

Once a day, or several times a day?

This is the question everyone asks first, and the honest answer is that it depends on what you want to learn.

Once a day

Best for most people starting out. Log in the evening, or whenever you have a reliable quiet minute, and rate the day as a whole. It is the easiest habit to keep, and a single daily point is plenty to reveal weekly rhythms, the effect of sleep, and slow drifts over a season. The trade-off is recency bias: a rough hour at 9pm colors the whole day's rating. Naming the moment in your note ("fine until the evening") keeps that honest.

Twice a day

Morning and evening. This catches diurnal patterns that a single entry hides — mornings that are consistently heavier than evenings, or the reverse. It is the natural next step once daily logging feels automatic.

Several times, or whenever something shifts

Most useful if you are trying to understand mood swings or anxiety spikes, where the interesting information is the change itself, not the daily average. Log at the shift, tag what was happening, and keep the entry short. Be aware of the trade-off in the other direction: checking in constantly can tip from observing your mood into monitoring it anxiously. If tracking starts to feel like surveillance, dial back to once a day.

A reasonable plan: two weeks of once-daily entries, then look at what you have and decide whether you need finer resolution. Most people do not.

Factors worth tagging

Tagging factors is what makes the record explanatory rather than merely descriptive. You do not need all of these — pick four or five that plausibly matter for you and stay consistent. Consistency is what makes comparison possible later.

Two cautions. First, more tags is not better: ten factors tracked sloppily are worth less than four tracked consistently. Second, a factor that appears alongside low mood is a lead to follow, not proof of a cause — the mood chart guide covers the difference in detail.

Why consistency beats detail

The most common way mood tracking fails is not laziness. It is ambition. People start with long journal entries, keep it up for five days, miss one, feel behind, and stop. Thirty short entries are worth far more than six thoughtful ones, because patterns only exist across time.

A few rules that keep a record trustworthy:

  1. Keep the entry under thirty seconds. If it takes longer, you will skip it on exactly the days that matter most — the busy ones and the bad ones.
  2. Log the same way every time. Don't quietly redefine what a 4 means halfway through the month; your past self can't renegotiate.
  3. Don't backfill from memory. Reconstructing last Tuesday produces a plausible number rather than a true one. A gap in the record is honest; an invented entry is noise you can't identify later.
  4. Rate before you look back. Glancing at yesterday's number first nudges today's toward it.
  5. Treat a missed week as a missed week. Not a failed experiment. Pick it up the next day and the chart still works.

The habit sticks best when it's anchored to something you already do without thinking — brushing your teeth, putting your phone on to charge, the first coffee. A reminder at a consistent time carries the first couple of weeks; after that, the review is what keeps you going. Once a month, sit down with the chart for five minutes and look for one thing you didn't know. That five minutes is the entire payoff, and it's the step people skip.

Looking at a blank entry and drawing a blank is common too. A short list of mood journal prompts solves it — one question, one honest sentence, done.

Notebook vs app: an honest comparison

A paper notebook works. If you already keep one and you'll open it every evening, the rest of this section is optional. Paper has real advantages: nothing to install, no notification pulling you into a phone, and writing by hand slows you down in a way that suits reflective journaling.

What paper is bad at is the part that makes tracking pay off. It can't chart three months of ratings against your sleep tags, it isn't in your pocket at 11pm, and nobody totals a column of mood scores by factor twice. That's where an app earns its place:

  • An entry in about five seconds — tap the point on the 5-point scale, tap a factor or two, done. Add a note or a photo when you have more to say.
  • Charts and trends computed for you, including mood over time and mood broken down by the factors you tagged, so correlations surface without arithmetic.
  • Reminders at the time you choose, which is most of the battle in the first fortnight.
  • Apple Health sync and widgets, so the log lives alongside your other health data and is one tap from the home screen.
  • iCloud sync with no account and no email address — your entries stay yours, across iPhone, iPad and Mac.
  • Export to PDF, XLSX, CSV or JSON, which matters the day you want to bring three months of context to a therapist or doctor instead of trying to summarize it from memory in a fifteen-minute appointment.
  • AI insights over your own history — a free tier plus optional premium — that read back what your entries show. Useful as a second pair of eyes; it isn't advice, and it isn't a clinician.

One thing no tool does, on paper or on a screen: treat anything. A mood journal is a self-monitoring tool. It supports DBT-style self-observation and it can make a conversation with a professional dramatically more concrete, but it doesn't diagnose and it isn't therapy. Whichever you pick, the record you keep beats the perfect one you don't.

Frequently asked questions

How long before I see a pattern?

Usually two to four weeks for day-to-day patterns like sleep or exercise, and two to three months for anything cyclical or seasonal. If a week of entries shows you nothing, that's expected — keep going. The mood chart guide explains what to look for once you have enough data.

What if I genuinely can't tell how I feel?

Very common, and it gets easier with practice. Start with the body: tired, tense, restless, heavy, light. Then pick the closest word from an emotion wheel and let the number follow. "Numb" or "nothing much" is a real answer — log it as itself rather than forcing a rating you don't believe.

Should I track on good days too?

Yes, and this is the step most people skip. A log made only of bad days looks like an unbroken run of bad days, which is both discouraging and inaccurate. Good days are also where you find what actually helps — you can't repeat what you never recorded.

Can mood tracking make you dwell on your mood?

It can, if entries turn into long analyses of why you feel bad. Keep them short and factual, and treat the monthly review as the thinking part. If logging starts to feel like anxious monitoring rather than a quick note, reduce to once a day, or pause — the habit is meant to serve you.

Can I share this with my therapist or doctor?

Yes, and it's one of the best reasons to keep it. A few months of dated entries turns "I've been up and down" into something specific you can look at together. Export to PDF for a clean summary, or CSV/XLSX if they want the raw data.

A note on wellbeing

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. A mood journal is a self-tracking tool, not therapy and not a diagnostic instrument — it supports a conversation with a doctor or therapist rather than replacing one. If low mood lasts most of the day for more than two weeks, or you notice a lasting loss of interest, changes in sleep or appetite, or difficulty functioning day to day, please talk to a healthcare professional. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline in your country right away — support is available now.
