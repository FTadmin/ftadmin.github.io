How to Track Your Mood

Last updated: August 2026

Mood tracking is a small habit: rate how you feel on a simple scale at the same time each day, and note what's shaping it. One entry tells you almost nothing; the value arrives weeks later, when the record shows what you couldn't hold in your head — flattest days following short nights, or the "constant" low mood lifting by midweek.

Here's what to log, how often, which factors to tag, and why a plain record beats an elaborate one you abandon. After a few weeks, reading your mood chart picks up from there.

What to log in a single entry

A useful entry has four parts, and only the first is essential:

  • A rating. A 5-point scale is the sweet spot: very low, low, okay, good, very good. Rate how the last few hours actually felt, not how the day should have gone.
  • The time. A 3 at 7am is not a 3 at 11pm.
  • A factor or two. Sleep, work, exercise, a hard conversation. Tags turn a line on a chart into an explanation.
  • One line of text. "Slept badly, meeting ran long" brings the day back six weeks later. A photo works too.

Don't rate the day you wish you had — a rounded-up record will eventually mislead you. If naming the feeling is hard, the emotion wheel gets you from "bad" to something you can act on.

Once a day, or several times a day?

Once a day suits most people: log in the evening, rate the day as a whole. One daily point is enough to reveal weekly rhythms and the effect of sleep. The trade-off is recency bias — a rough hour at 9pm colors the whole rating, so name it in the note ("fine until the evening").

Twice a day catches mornings that run heavier than evenings. Log at every shift if you're chasing mood swings, where the change is the information — though constant check-ins can tip into anxious monitoring; if it feels like surveillance, dial back.

Start with two weeks once daily; most people never need more.

Why consistency beats detail

Mood tracking fails through ambition, not laziness: long entries for five days, one missed, stop. Thirty short entries beat six thoughtful ones — patterns only exist across time.

  1. Under thirty seconds, or you'll skip the busy days and the bad ones — the ones that matter.
  2. Log the same way every time. Don't quietly redefine what a 4 means mid-month.
  3. Don't backfill from memory. A gap is honest; an invented entry is noise.
  4. A missed week is a missed week, not a failed experiment.

Anchor the habit to something you already do — the first coffee, the phone on charge. The monthly five-minute review is the entire payoff, and the step people skip. If the blank entry stares back, mood journal prompts solve it.

A paper notebook works — nothing to install, no notifications, and handwriting suits reflection. But the payoff is charting three months of ratings against your sleep tags, and nobody does that by hand twice. The app takes the boring parts:

  • An entry in about five seconds — tap the scale, tap a factor, done.

No tool treats anything: a mood journal supports DBT-style self-observation and makes professional conversations concrete; it isn't therapy. The record you keep beats the perfect one you don't.

Frequently asked questions

How long before I see a pattern?

Usually two to four weeks for day-to-day patterns like sleep or exercise, and two to three months for anything cyclical or seasonal. A week of entries showing nothing is expected — keep going. The mood chart guide covers what to look for once you have enough data.

What if I genuinely can't tell how I feel?

Very common, and it improves with practice. Start with the body: tired, tense, restless, heavy. Then pick the closest word from an emotion wheel and let the number follow. "Numb" is a real answer — log it as itself rather than forcing a rating you don't believe.

Should I track on good days too?

Yes — this is the step most people skip. A log made only of bad days reads as an unbroken run of bad days, which is discouraging and inaccurate. Good days are also where you find what helps; you can't repeat what you never recorded.

Can mood tracking make you dwell on your mood?

It can, if entries become long analyses of why you feel bad. Keep them short and factual, and save the thinking for the monthly review. If logging starts to feel like anxious monitoring, drop to once a day, or pause — the habit is meant to serve you.

Can I share this with my therapist or doctor?

Yes — it's one of the best reasons to keep it. A few months of dated entries turns "I've been up and down" into something specific you can look at together. Export a PDF for a clean summary, or CSV/XLSX for the raw data.

A note on wellbeing

This article is for general information only and is not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. A mood journal is a self-tracking tool, not therapy and not a diagnostic instrument — it supports a conversation with a doctor or therapist rather than replacing one. If low mood lasts most of the day for more than two weeks, or you notice a lasting loss of interest, changes in sleep or appetite, or difficulty functioning day to day, please talk to a healthcare professional. If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis helpline in your country right away — support is available now.
